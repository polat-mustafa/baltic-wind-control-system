# Chapter 2: The Millwrights: Holland, England, and the Birth of Mechanical Power

*The rain came sideways at half past one, streaking the porthole glass and reducing the Baltic to a grey-on-grey abstraction. Kaan barely noticed. He was reading about the Netherlands.*

*Not the Netherlands he had visited once as a tourist — the canals and the bicycles and the museums. The other Netherlands, the one that should not exist. Twenty-six percent of the country lies below sea level. Not a sliver along the coast — a full quarter of the nation, including Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and The Hague, sitting in a shallow bowl that the North Sea would reclaim in a matter of weeks if the pumps ever stopped. Every day, around the clock, electric pumping stations lift millions of cubic meters of water from the polders and push it back into the rivers and the sea. Before there were electric pumps, there were diesel pumps. Before diesel, steam. And before steam, for over four hundred years, there were windmills.*

*The article on Kaan's tablet had a photograph of nineteen windmills standing in a row along a canal, their sails motionless against a low Dutch sky. Kinderdijk, the caption said — built between 1738 and 1740 to drain the Alblasserwaard polder, and still maintained in working order as a backup. A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1997. Nineteen machines, nearly three centuries old, that had kept a nation from drowning.*

*Kaan thought about the turbines he was sailing toward — thirty-four of them, each 260 meters to blade tip, extracting energy from the same medium that those Kinderdijk mills had used. Different scale, different physics, different purpose. But the same fundamental idea: a rotor in the wind, doing work. He scrolled down to a section about how the European windmill was invented, and discovered, to his surprise, that nobody really knows.*

## 2.1 The European Reinvention: Post Mills and Tower Mills

The windmill was invented twice.

The first time was in Persia, as we saw in Chapter 1 — a vertical-axis drag device, elegant in its simplicity, limited in its efficiency. The second time was in northwestern Europe, sometime in the late 12th century, and it was a completely different machine. [1]

The European windmill was a horizontal-axis device: the rotor turned in a vertical plane, perpendicular to the wind, mounted on a horizontal shaft. This was not an incremental improvement on the Persian design. It was a reinvention from first principles. The sails were tilted and twisted so that the wind did not merely push them (drag) but flowed over them to create a pressure difference (lift) — the same aerodynamic principle that allows an airplane wing to generate upward force. The result was a machine that could, in theory, extract far more energy from the wind than any drag-based device. [2]

The earliest known European windmill appeared in England in 1185, recorded in a rental agreement for a post mill at Weedley, Yorkshire. [3] By 1191, a Papal decree referenced windmills in the Normandy region. Within a century, they had spread across England, France, Flanders, and the German states. The Crusaders may have encountered Persian windmills in the Middle East and brought the *concept* of wind-powered milling back to Europe, but the *machine* they built bore no resemblance to the Seistan design. Whether the European windmill was inspired by, or entirely independent of, the Persian one remains an open question among historians of technology. [4]

The first European type was the **post mill**. In a post mill, the entire body of the mill — a rectangular wooden box called the "buck," containing the millstones and all the gearing — is balanced on a single massive vertical oak post. To face the wind, the miller physically pushed the entire structure around the post, using a long tailpole as a lever. It was effective but laborious: every time the wind shifted, the miller had to stop work, climb down, and wrestle several tons of wood into a new orientation. [5]

The **tower mill** solved this problem. Developed in France by the early 14th century, the tower mill separated the body from the rotor. The main structure was a fixed cylindrical tower, built of stone or brick, tall enough to reach above surrounding trees and buildings. Only the top — a rotating wooden cap carrying the sails and the windshaft — needed to turn. The miller adjusted the cap's direction by means of a lever or a chain, leaving the machinery floors undisturbed. [6]

The fixed tower had a second advantage: it could be built taller, wider, and stronger than a post mill. Multiple floors could house different stages of milling — grain storage above, millstones in the middle, flour bagging below. The tower mill was, in effect, a small factory.

The **smock mill**, originating in the Netherlands in the early 16th century, was a hybrid. Like the tower mill, only its cap rotated. But instead of heavy masonry, the body was a timber-framed octagonal structure clad in weather-boarding — lighter than stone, suitable for the soft, wet soils of the Dutch lowlands where a masonry tower would sink. [7] The smock mill became the workhorse of Dutch drainage, and its distinctive tapering silhouette is the image most people picture when they think of a windmill.

## 2.2 Holland's Fight Against the Sea

The Netherlands did not always need windmills. In the early Middle Ages, the land was higher — or rather, the land had not yet compacted. The peat bogs that covered much of the western Netherlands were spongy and saturated but above sea level. As the Dutch dug drainage ditches and extracted peat for fuel, the land surface dropped. By the 14th century, large areas of Holland and Zeeland had sunk below the high-water mark of the surrounding rivers and the North Sea. The country was, quite literally, digging itself into a hole. [8]

The solution was the **polder**: a parcel of land enclosed by dikes, from which water is continuously pumped to keep the interior dry. The earliest polders relied on gravity drainage — sluice gates that opened at low tide to let water flow out. But as the land sank further, gravity was no longer enough. The water had to be actively lifted.

Windmill-powered drainage began in earnest in the 15th century. The first large-scale reclamation project was the **Beemster Polder**, drained between 1607 and 1612 using forty-two windmills arranged around a ring canal of 48.9 kilometers. The project, overseen by the hydraulic engineer Jan Adriaanszoon Leeghwater, converted 7,208 hectares of lake into farmland — an area roughly the size of Manhattan. It was so successful, and so astonishing, that the Beemster's geometric landscape was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999. [9]

The drainage mill used a **scoop wheel** — a large paddle wheel, typically 5 to 7 meters in diameter, mounted at the base of the mill and partially submerged in the polder's main drainage canal. As the wind turned the sails, a series of gears transmitted the rotation downward to the scoop wheel, which lifted water from the polder and deposited it into the ring canal at a higher level. Each mill could lift water about 1 to 1.5 meters. To drain land that sat several meters below the ring canal, the Dutch arranged mills in series — a **molengang**, or "mill gang" — where each mill lifted water to an intermediate canal, and the next mill lifted it again. [10]

The power required to lift water against gravity is given by:

$$
P_{pump} = \rho_w \cdot g \cdot Q \cdot H
$$

where:
- $P_{pump}$ = hydraulic power required [W]
- $\rho_w$ = density of water ≈ 1,000 [kg/m³]
- $g$ = gravitational acceleration ≈ 9.81 [m/s²]
- $Q$ = volume flow rate of water [m³/s]
- $H$ = height the water must be lifted (head) [m]

This formula makes the trade-off visible. To pump more water ($Q$), you need more power. To lift it higher ($H$), you need more power. The Dutch solved both problems by building more mills and arranging them in cascades. By the 18th century, the Netherlands had an estimated 9,000 to 10,000 windmills — the densest concentration of wind energy infrastructure the world had ever seen, or would see again until the 21st century. [11]

The nineteen windmills at Kinderdijk, built between 1738 and 1740 to drain the Alblasserwaard polder, are the most famous surviving example of this system. Eight mills of the Nederwaard and eight of the Overwaard, plus three additional drainage mills, still stand along the canal — maintained in working order as a backup for the electric pumping stations that replaced them. [12]

## 2.3 The Zaanstreek: Wind-Powered Industry

The Dutch did not only use windmills to pump water. They used them to build an empire.

The **Zaanstreek** (Zaan district), a strip of land along the Zaan River north of Amsterdam, became the oldest industrial area in Western Europe — and it ran almost entirely on wind. At its peak in the 18th century, approximately 600 windmills operated simultaneously in the district, producing sawn timber, paper, linseed oil, rapeseed oil, flour, spices, dyes, processed fibers, and cocoa powder. [13]

The catalyst was a single invention. In December 1593, a Zaan craftsman named Cornelis Corneliszoon van Uitgeest was granted a patent for a **crankshaft** mechanism that converted the rotary motion of a windmill into the reciprocating (up-and-down) motion needed to drive a saw blade. He built his first wind-powered sawmill in 1594. The effect was staggering: a wind-powered sawmill could convert timber into planks roughly **thirty times faster** than a team of manual pit-sawyers. [14]

This was not a marginal improvement. It was an industrial revolution — two centuries before the one we normally capitalize. By 1630, just thirty-four years after Corneliszoon's first sawmill, eighty-three wind-powered sawmills were operating north of Amsterdam. [15] Timber floated down the Rhine from the Black Forest and arrived by ship from the Baltic. The sawmills converted it into planks at a rate no other country could match.

The planks built ships. The Dutch *fluyt* — a cargo vessel designed for maximum hold volume with minimum crew — could be mass-produced because the timber supply was mechanized. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) and the West India Company (WIC) grew not on courage alone but on a logistics chain that started with a windmill on the Zaan River. [16] The connection between wind power and global trade was direct: without Corneliszoon's crankshaft, the Dutch Golden Age would have looked very different.

The Zaanstreek also pioneered something that would not have a name for centuries: **supply chain integration**. Raw materials arrived by barge. Different mills processed them in sequence — logs became planks, seeds became oil, grain became flour. Finished products shipped out by the same waterways. The district was, in modern terms, an industrial cluster powered by distributed renewable energy.

## 2.4 The Fantail and the Spring Sail: Control Before Computers

A windmill has two operational problems that a water wheel does not: the wind changes direction, and the wind changes speed.

The first problem — changing direction — was solved manually for centuries. In a post mill, the miller pushed the entire body around its central post. In a tower or smock mill, the miller turned the cap using a chain or a long pole. Either way, it required constant attention. A miller who fell asleep during a wind shift would find his sails edge-on to the wind, producing no power. Worse, a sudden shift could catch the sails on their back — the dreaded "tailwinding" — which could destroy the gearing, crack the windshaft, or tear the sails apart. [17]

In 1745, Edmund Lee, a blacksmith working at Brock Mill forge on the Haigh estate in Lancashire, England, patented a device that made this problem disappear. He called it the **fantail**. [18]

The fantail is a small auxiliary rotor — a ring of five to eight vanes — mounted behind the main sails, perpendicular to them. When the main sails are correctly facing the wind, the fantail vanes are edge-on to it and do not turn. But when the wind shifts, it strikes the fantail at an angle, spinning the vanes. Through a gear train connected to wheels running on a track around the cap, the fantail's rotation drives the cap around until the main sails are once again square to the wind. At that point, the fantail is edge-on again and stops. [19]

It is, in the language of modern control engineering, a **negative feedback loop**: a deviation from the desired state (sails not facing the wind) produces a corrective action (cap rotation) that drives the system back toward the desired state. The fantail is a proportional controller — the greater the wind's angle of attack on the vanes, the faster they spin, the faster the cap corrects. Edmund Lee, a blacksmith who probably never heard the word "feedback," had invented automatic yaw control 250 years before the first wind turbine used an electric yaw motor.

The second problem — changing wind speed — was solved in stages. Traditional sailcloth was a disaster in gusty conditions. A sudden increase in wind speed could tear the canvas, snap the windshaft, or set the millstones spinning fast enough to overheat and catch fire (grain dust is explosive). The miller's only option was to stop the mill, climb the tower, and adjust or remove the sails by hand — a dangerous job, especially in the storms that made adjustment most urgent.

In 1772, Andrew Meikle, a Scottish millwright working at Houston Mill on the Phantassie Estate in East Lothian, replaced the traditional sailcloth with a system of hinged wooden shutters, similar to a Venetian blind. Each shutter was connected to a spring. In high winds, the pressure on the shutters compressed the springs and the shutters opened, spilling excess wind. In lighter winds, the springs held the shutters closed, capturing more energy. [20] Meikle had invented the first automatic power-regulation system for a wind machine.

Meikle's spring sails had a limitation: each spring had to be set individually with the mill at rest. In 1807, the English engineer Sir William Cubitt combined Meikle's shutters with a remote-control mechanism — a chain and striking rod running through the hollow windshaft — that allowed the miller to adjust all the shutters simultaneously while the mill was running. [21] Cubitt's "patent sail" was the state of the art in wind energy technology for the next century.

## 2.5 Oliver Evans and the Automated Mill

The windmill's final contribution to the history of technology came not from Europe but from the young United States — and it had nothing to do with the wind itself.

In 1784, Oliver Evans, a self-taught engineer in Delaware, looked at a flour mill and saw waste. Not material waste — *human* waste. A typical mill required four men to move grain and flour between stages: one to pour grain into the hopper, one to collect meal from the millstones, one to spread it for cooling, and one to bag the finished product. The milling itself was mechanized; the material handling was not. [22]

Evans redesigned the entire process. At his mill on Red Clay Creek near Newport, Delaware, he connected every stage with mechanical conveyors — **bucket elevators** to lift grain vertically, **Archimedean screws** to move it horizontally, **conveyor belts** to transport it between floors, and a device he called the **hopper-boy**, a rotating rake that spread hot meal in a circle, cooling and collecting it automatically. Grain entered at one end; finished flour emerged at the other. No human touched the product between loading and bagging. [23]

It was the first continuous production line in any industry, anywhere in the world. Henry Ford would not build his automobile assembly line for another 130 years.

Evans described the system in *The Young Mill-Wright and Miller's Guide*, self-published in Philadelphia in 1795. The book went through fifteen editions between 1795 and 1860 and became the standard reference for American mill construction. [24] George Washington himself purchased a license in 1790 and had Evans's brothers install the automated system at the Mount Vernon gristmill by 1791. [25]

The lesson for our story is this: even before the wind turbine existed, the windmill had already demonstrated every principle that would define modern energy infrastructure. Distributed generation (thousands of mills, each serving local demand). Mechanical-to-useful-work conversion. Automatic control (the fantail, the spring sail). And now, with Evans, process automation — the elimination of human labor from repetitive tasks through mechanical integration.

The millwrights of Holland and England did not merely grind grain and pump water. They invented an industrial paradigm. The wind turbine engineers of the 21st century inherited it.

## 2.X Worked Example: How Much Water Can a Dutch Drainage Mill Pump?

Consider a typical Dutch smock mill used for polder drainage, and calculate its pumping capacity.

**Mill parameters:**
- Rotor diameter: 20 m → swept area $A = \frac{\pi}{4}(20)^2 \approx 314$ m²
- Mean wind speed in the western Netherlands: 7 m/s
- Air density at sea level: $\rho_{air} = 1.225$ kg/m³
- Coefficient of performance (18th-century lift-based sails): $C_p \approx 0.25$
- Scoop wheel mechanical efficiency: $\eta_{scoop} \approx 0.50$
- Lift height (polder to ring canal): $H = 1.5$ m

**Step 1: Mechanical power from the wind.**

$$
P_{mech} = \frac{1}{2} \rho_{air} A v^3 \cdot C_p = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.225 \times 314 \times 7^3 \times 0.25
$$

$$
P_{mech} = 0.6125 \times 314 \times 343 \times 0.25 = 16{,}490 \text{ W} \approx 16.5 \text{ kW}
$$

**Step 2: Hydraulic pumping power available (after scoop wheel losses).**

$$
P_{hydraulic} = P_{mech} \times \eta_{scoop} = 16{,}500 \times 0.50 = 8{,}250 \text{ W}
$$

**Step 3: Volume flow rate of water pumped.**

Rearranging the pumping power equation:

$$
Q = \frac{P_{hydraulic}}{\rho_w \cdot g \cdot H} = \frac{8{,}250}{1{,}000 \times 9.81 \times 1.5} = \frac{8{,}250}{14{,}715} = 0.56 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}
$$

That is **560 liters per second**, or roughly **2,000 cubic meters per hour**.

**Step 4: Beemster Polder check.**

The Beemster Polder (7,208 hectares) was drained by 42 windmills. Annual rainfall in the Netherlands averages about 800 mm. Assuming each mill operates about 200 days per year, 8 hours per day (wind permitting):

$$
V_{annual} = 42 \times 2{,}000 \text{ m}^3/\text{hr} \times 8 \text{ hr/day} \times 200 \text{ days} = 134.4 \times 10^6 \text{ m}^3
$$

The annual rainfall on the Beemster:

$$
V_{rain} = 7{,}208 \times 10^4 \text{ m}^2 \times 0.80 \text{ m} = 57.7 \times 10^6 \text{ m}^3
$$

The 42 mills had more than twice the capacity needed for rainfall alone — the margin covered groundwater seepage, storm surges, and the inconvenient fact that wind does not blow on schedule. Overdesigning the drainage system was not inefficiency. It was survival.

## Key Takeaways

- **The European windmill was a reinvention, not an evolution.** The horizontal-axis, lift-based European design was fundamentally different from the vertical-axis, drag-based Persian windmill. Whether the concept traveled from east to west is debated; the engineering did not.

- **The Netherlands exists because of windmills.** Twenty-six percent of the country is below sea level. For over four centuries, wind-powered drainage mills kept the polders dry. At the system's peak, 9,000 to 10,000 windmills operated simultaneously across the country.

- **Wind powered the first industrial district.** The Zaanstreek's 600 windmills — producing sawn timber, paper, oil, and flour — constituted an industrial cluster powered by distributed renewable energy, two centuries before the concept had a name. Corneliszoon's 1593 crankshaft enabled sawmills 30 times faster than manual labor, directly fueling the Dutch Golden Age.

- **Automatic control predates computers by centuries.** Lee's fantail (1745) is a negative feedback yaw controller. Meikle's spring sail (1772) is an automatic power limiter. Cubitt's patent sail (1807) added remote adjustability. These are the ancestors of every yaw motor and pitch actuator on a modern turbine.

- **Process automation started in a flour mill.** Oliver Evans's 1784 continuous production line at Red Clay Creek — grain in, flour out, no human handling in between — predated Ford's assembly line by 130 years and demonstrated that mechanical systems could eliminate entire categories of labor.

*Kaan set down the tablet and stared at the porthole. The rain had stopped. Through the streaked glass, the sea looked calmer — still grey, still cold, but the whitecaps had flattened into a long, sullen swell.*

*He was thinking about the fantail. A blacksmith in Lancashire had solved automatic yaw control in 1745 with a ring of wooden vanes and a gear train. No sensors, no processors, no PID loop — just physics. When the wind shifted, the vanes turned. When the cap was aligned, the vanes stopped. Negative feedback, implemented in oak and iron. And here Kaan was, about to work on a wind farm where each turbine had three electric yaw motors, a wind vane, an anemometer, and a yaw controller running several hundred lines of code — all to accomplish exactly the same thing Edmund Lee's fantail had done with zero lines of code and zero watts of external power.*

*He thought about Anders's reading list and understood, now, why it started with history. Not because the history was quaint or interesting — though it was both — but because the problems had not changed. Face the wind. Regulate the power. Survive the storm. Every turbine engineer in the 21st century was solving the same problems that the millwrights of Holland and England had solved with wood and iron and ingenuity. The tools were different. The physics was the same.*

[21] Encyclopaedia Britannica. "Windmill." Sir William Cubitt (1807) combined Meikle's shutters with a chain-and-striking-rod mechanism through the hollow windshaft, enabling the miller to adjust all shutters simultaneously while the mill was running — the "patent sail."

[22] Encyclopaedia Britannica. "Oliver Evans." Evans (1755-1819) was a self-taught engineer who conceived the automated mill system circa 1784-1785, building it at Red Clay Creek near Newport, Delaware.

[23] U.S. National Park Service. "Oliver Evans." The system integrated bucket elevators, Archimedean screws, conveyor belts, and the hopper-boy (a rotating rake for cooling meal), creating the first continuous production line. It reduced the mill workforce from four men to one.

[24] Evans, O. (1795). *The Young Mill-Wright and Miller's Guide.* Philadelphia. Self-published in an edition of 2,000 copies; the book went through 15 editions between 1795 and 1860, becoming the standard American reference for mill construction.

[25] George Washington's Mount Vernon. "The Magnificent Millworks of Oliver Evans." Washington purchased an Evans license in 1790, and Evans's brothers supervised the installation at Mount Vernon's gristmill by 1791.
