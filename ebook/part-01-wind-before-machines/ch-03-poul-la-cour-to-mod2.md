# Chapter 3: From Poul la Cour to MOD-2: Electricity from Wind

*The aft deck of the CTV was loud — diesel exhaust, wave slap, the heavy clunk of fenders against the hull — but Kaan barely heard any of it. He was staring at the horizon.*

*They had appeared ten minutes ago, at first just a flicker in the haze, like flagpoles without flags. Then, as the vessel closed the distance, they resolved into something his brain struggled to accept. Towers. White, impossibly tall, rising from a sea that had no business supporting structures of that scale. And at the top of each tower, three blades turning in a slow, silent arc against the grey Baltic sky.*

*"First time?" The voice came from his left. Anders — the senior electrical engineer Kaan had been assigned to shadow — was leaning on the rail, lifejacket zipped to his chin, watching Kaan's face with the faint amusement of someone who had seen this reaction before.*

*"They're bigger than I expected," Kaan said, and immediately felt stupid. He had read the specifications. He knew the rotor diameter was 236 meters. He knew the hub height was 150 meters. He had done the maths, drawn the diagrams, passed the exams. None of it had prepared him for the physical reality: thirty-four machines standing in the open sea, each taller than any building he had ever entered, turning with a calm authority that made the storm he had just endured feel trivial.*

*"Two hundred and thirty-six meters, tip to tip," Anders said. "Each blade is longer than a football pitch. The nacelle up there weighs six hundred tonnes. And the whole thing is balanced on a single steel tube driven into the seabed." He paused. "But you know the strange part? The idea — a rotor in the wind, connected to a generator — hasn't changed since 1891. A Danish schoolteacher figured it out with a wooden windmill and a dynamo. Everything since then has been engineering."*

*Kaan looked at the nearest turbine. Its blades caught the low sun and threw a moving shadow across the water. A rotor in the wind, connected to a generator. 1891.*

*"Tell me about the schoolteacher," he said.*

## 3.1 Poul la Cour and the Dawn of Wind Electricity

Before the schoolteacher, there were two inventors who proved the concept — almost by accident.

In July 1887, James Blyth, a professor of natural philosophy at Anderson's College in Glasgow (now the University of Strathclyde), erected a wind turbine in the garden of his holiday cottage at Marykirk, a village in northeast Scotland. The machine was modest: a 10-meter wooden tower supporting four canvas sails, each about 2.4 meters long, driving a dynamo through a vertical shaft and flywheel. The electricity charged twelve Fauré accumulators — early lead-acid batteries — which powered the lights in Blyth's cottage. It was the first house in the world illuminated by wind-generated electricity. [1]

Blyth offered to light Marykirk's main street with his surplus power. The villagers refused. They considered electricity "the work of the devil." [2] Undeterred, Blyth patented his "wind engine" in 1891 (British patent no. 19,401) and installed a larger, improved turbine at the Montrose Royal Lunatic Asylum, where it operated successfully for several decades — a fitting footnote in the history of technologies initially dismissed as madness. [3]

Across the Atlantic, the American industrialist Charles F. Brush had a bigger budget and a bigger idea. In the winter of 1887-1888, Brush erected an enormous windmill on the grounds of his mansion in Cleveland, Ohio: a 60-foot (18-meter) tower supporting a 56-foot (17-meter) diameter rotor with 144 cedar blades, the whole structure weighing 80,000 pounds (36 tonnes). The rotor drove a 12 kW DC dynamo through a belt-and-pulley system with a 50:1 gear ratio. Twelve batteries in the basement stored the output, which illuminated Brush's home and powered roughly 100 incandescent lamps. The "Brush Windmill Dynamo" ran for twenty years and was featured in *Scientific American* on December 20, 1890. [4]

But neither Blyth nor Brush created a movement. Blyth was an academic whose neighbours feared his invention. Brush was a wealthy industrialist whose machine was a personal curiosity, not a scalable technology. The person who turned wind electricity from a novelty into a system was a Danish physicist named Poul la Cour.

**Poul la Cour** (1846–1908) was a physicist, meteorologist, and instructor at the Askov Folk High School in southern Jutland — part of the Danish *folkehøjskole* tradition, where adult students studied practical arts and sciences. In the summer of 1891, la Cour built his first experimental windmill at Askov. By 1895, the village was illuminated entirely by wind-generated electricity — the first community in the world to achieve this. In 1897, he built a larger "Dutch-style" experimental mill with improved sails and instrumentation. [5]

What distinguished la Cour from every predecessor was his approach: he treated wind energy as a scientific problem, not a mechanical curiosity. He built a wind tunnel — one of the first in Scandinavia — to test different sail and blade configurations under controlled conditions. He measured power output systematically. He published his results. [6]

La Cour's most important invention was the **kratostat** — a differential regulator that maintained a steady electrical output despite variable wind speeds. Every windmill operator knew the problem: the wind gusted and lulled, and so did the voltage. Flickering lights were annoying; flickering arc lamps were dangerous. The kratostat sensed speed deviations and adjusted the electrical load to smooth the output. It was adopted widely in Scandinavian and German wind-electric installations and remained in use for decades. [7]

He also experimented with **energy storage via hydrogen**. Using surplus wind electricity, la Cour ran an electrolyser that split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen was stored in tanks and burned in limelight (calcium light) lamps to illuminate the Askov school when the wind was calm. It was, in principle, the same power-to-gas concept that 21st-century engineers are pursuing with offshore wind and green hydrogen — implemented in 1895 with glass jars and a Bunsen burner. [8]

But la Cour's greatest contribution was institutional, not technical. In 1903, he founded the **Danish Wind Electricity Society** (*Dansk Vindelektricitet Selskab*, DVES) and began publishing a bimonthly journal on wind electricity, writing most of the articles himself. He trained approximately twenty electricians per year at Askov, teaching them not only how to build and maintain wind-electric systems but *why* each component worked the way it did. In the five years following the society's founding, the DVES consulting engineer planned roughly one hundred small electricity plants across rural Denmark — a third of them powered by wind. [9]

La Cour died in 1908, but his school did not. One of his students, a young man named **Johannes Juul**, would spend the next fifty years refining the ideas he had learned at Askov. In 1957, Juul would build the Gedser wind turbine — a 200 kW, three-bladed machine that ran for eleven years without maintenance and became the direct ancestor of every modern wind turbine in the world. That story belongs to section 3.4. But the thread from la Cour to Juul to the modern wind industry is arguably the most important lineage in renewable energy history.

## 3.2 The Jacobs Wind Electric: Power on the Prairie

The path from Blyth's cottage to the modern wind farm ran through an unlikely place: a cattle ranch in Montana.

In 1922, brothers Marcellus and Joseph Jacobs were growing up on their family's ranch near Vida, Montana — a town so remote that the nearest power line was a hundred miles away. Like thousands of rural American families, the Jacobses had no electricity. They had been experimenting with small wind-driven generators since their teens, and they faced the same problem la Cour had faced in Denmark: how to get a steady, reliable electrical supply from a source that gusted and died without warning. [10]

After several years of testing different rotor designs, the Jacobs brothers settled on something that set their machines apart from every wind-driven generator before them: a **three-bladed propeller** based on aviation aerodynamics. Where Brush had used 144 blades to maximise torque at low speeds — the approach inherited from water-pumping windmills — the Jacobs propeller used just three slender, twisted blades to maximise rotational speed. [11]

The reason involves a concept called the **tip speed ratio**:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\omega \cdot R}{v}
$$

where:
- $\lambda$ = tip speed ratio [dimensionless]
- $\omega$ = angular velocity of the rotor [rad/s]
- $R$ = rotor radius [m]
- $v$ = wind speed [m/s]

The tip speed ratio measures how fast the blade tips move relative to the incoming wind. A water-pumping windmill, with its many broad blades, operates at $\lambda \approx 1$ — the tips move at roughly the same speed as the wind. This produces high torque (useful for driving a pump) but low rotational speed (useless for driving a generator, which needs hundreds of revolutions per minute). A three-bladed electricity-generating rotor operates at $\lambda \approx 6$ to $8$ — the tips move six to eight times faster than the wind. Less torque, much more speed, and crucially, a better match for the electrical generator on the other end of the shaft. [12]

The Jacobs machine also incorporated a **flyball governor** — a centrifugal device borrowed from steam engine technology — that automatically adjusted the blade pitch as wind speed changed. In high winds, the governor rotated the blades toward feather (reducing the angle of attack), limiting the rotor speed and protecting the generator. In light winds, the blades pitched back to capture more energy. It was Meikle's spring sail reborn in Chapter 2 — automatic power regulation, now implemented with precision engineering rather than hinged shutters and coil springs. [13]

In 1931, the brothers moved production to Minneapolis and began manufacturing improved wind-electric systems for sale through dealer networks across the United States, Canada, Mexico, and eventually every continent. Their products ranged from 1 to 3 kW — enough to power lights, a radio, and a few small appliances for a homestead. The popular Model 45 (45-amp output) sold for $290 for the generator assembly, $365 for a 50-foot tower, and $175 for a 440-amp-hour battery bank — a total of roughly $830, equivalent to about $18,000 today. [14]

The Jacobs machines were renowned for their reliability. The company's **Master Mind** control panel regulated battery charging so precisely that Jacobs could offer an unconditional ten-year warranty on the batteries — more than three times the industry standard. Before production ceased in the late 1950s, approximately 20,000 Jacobs wind-electric systems had shipped from Minneapolis. [15]

What killed the Jacobs wind plant was not a better technology. It was the grid.

The **Rural Electrification Act of 1936** created the Rural Electrification Administration (REA), which lent money to cooperatives to build power lines into the American countryside. By the early 1950s, over 90 percent of American farms had grid electricity. The economics were merciless: a farmer who had paid $830 for a Jacobs system that provided a few kilowatts in good wind could now connect to the grid for a fixed monthly rate and run as many appliances as the wire could carry. The wind plants were dismounted, sold for scrap, or left standing on their towers as curiosities. Jacobs Wind Electric ceased production by the mid-1950s. [16]

The lesson was stark, and it would echo for decades: wind energy could not compete with centralised fossil-fuel generation on cost. Not yet.

## 3.3 Smith-Putnam: The Giant of Grandpa's Knob

Palmer Cosslett Putnam had a problem that money could solve but economics would not.

In the late 1930s, Putnam — an MIT-trained engineer with a house on Cape Cod — was frustrated by the cost of electricity. The Cape got plenty of wind. Putnam understood the cubic relationship between wind speed and power. He began to wonder: could a single large wind turbine, connected to the utility grid, produce electricity cheaply enough to compete with coal? [17]

Putnam's answer was to think big — radically, absurdly big.

In 1937, he approached General Electric, which passed. He then found a willing partner in the **S. Morgan Smith Company** of York, Pennsylvania, a manufacturer of large hydroelectric turbines. Smith agreed to fund and build the machine. The engineering team included Vannevar Bush (then dean of MIT's School of Engineering) as an adviser. [18]

The result was the **Smith-Putnam wind turbine**, erected on a mountain called Grandpa's Knob near Rutland, Vermont, at an elevation of 610 meters (2,000 feet). Its specifications were extraordinary for 1941:

- **Rated power:** 1.25 MW
- **Rotor diameter:** 53 meters (175 feet) — two blades, stainless steel
- **Tower height:** 30 meters (100 feet) — welded steel lattice
- **Total weight:** approximately 36 tonnes (80,000 pounds)
- **Each blade:** 8 tons, approximately 20 meters long

On **October 19, 1941**, in gusty 25-mph winds above Lake Champlain, the Smith-Putnam turbine was synchronised with the Central Vermont Public Service Corporation's grid. It was the first time in history that wind-generated electricity had been fed into a utility's high-voltage transmission lines. [19]

The machine operated intermittently for the next three and a half years, accumulating approximately 1,100 hours of operation and generating 298,240 kilowatt-hours of electricity. The cost of electricity was well above the price of coal-fired power, but the machine proved that a megawatt-scale wind turbine could operate in a grid environment. [20]

Then, at 3:10 a.m. on **March 26, 1945**, it stopped working forever.

One of the eight-ton blades broke at a previously repaired weak point in the spar and was hurled 230 meters (750 feet) through the darkness. The operator on duty was thrown off his feet by the sudden imbalance. The remaining blade and nacelle shook violently until the emergency brake engaged. [21]

The root cause was known before it happened. The blade spar had developed fatigue cracks during operation, but wartime steel shortages made a full repair impossible. A temporary fix had held for months — then failed under cyclic loading in a Vermont gale. With the war still consuming the nation's industrial capacity and the project over budget, the S. Morgan Smith Company declined to build a replacement. The turbine was dismantled in 1946.

Putnam published his engineering analysis as *Power from the Wind* in 1948 — a rigorous, 224-page monograph that documented everything: the site selection methodology, the aerodynamic design, the structural analysis, the blade failure, and the economic viability calculations. It remained the definitive reference on large wind turbines for the next three decades and directly informed every subsequent government wind programme. [22]

## 3.4 NASA's MOD Programme

After Smith-Putnam's blade shattered on Grandpa's Knob, large-scale wind energy development went quiet for nearly three decades. Oil was cheap. Coal was abundant. Nuclear power promised electricity "too cheap to meter." There was no economic or political reason to invest in a technology that had failed spectacularly on a Vermont mountain.

The one exception was Denmark.

In 1957, **Johannes Juul** — an electrical engineer who had been one of Poul la Cour's last students at Askov — completed the **Gedser wind turbine** on the southern coast of the Danish island of Falster. Funded by the Danske Elværkers Forening (Danish Utilities Association) with Marshall Plan support, the Gedser machine was remarkably modern in concept: [23]

- **Rated power:** 200 kW
- **Rotor diameter:** 24 meters — three blades, upwind configuration
- **Regulation:** Aerodynamic stall (passive — no pitch mechanism)
- **Generator:** Asynchronous (induction) — crucial for simple grid connection
- **Emergency braking:** Centrifugal tip brakes (aerodynamic)
- **Construction:** Steel spars, aluminium shells, wooden ribs

The Gedser turbine ran from 1957 to 1967 — eleven years — without significant maintenance, generating an average of approximately 220,000 kWh per year in winds averaging 6.3 m/s at hub height. [24] Its three-bladed upwind layout, asynchronous generator, and stall regulation became the template for what the wind industry would later call the **Danish concept** — the design philosophy that dominates the global market to this day. Prior wind generators, from Blyth to Smith-Putnam, had used DC dynamos or synchronous generators. Juul's choice of an asynchronous (induction) generator was a leap: it was simpler, more robust, required no synchronisation equipment, and inherently locked its frequency to the grid.

Then, on October 17, 1973, the **Organisation of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries** declared an oil embargo against nations supporting Israel in the Yom Kippur War. The price of oil quadrupled in months. Suddenly, every government in the West wanted alternatives.

In the United States, the response was the **Federal Wind Energy Program**, managed jointly by NASA's Lewis Research Center (now Glenn Research Center) in Cleveland, Ohio, and the Energy Research and Development Administration (ERDA, later absorbed into the Department of Energy). The programme's ambition was to build progressively larger wind turbines, each generation learning from the last, until the technology could compete with fossil fuels. [25]

The machines were designated **MOD** (for "modification" — each generation modified the design of the last):

**MOD-0** (1975): A 100 kW test bed installed at NASA's Plum Brook Station in Sandusky, Ohio. Two blades, 38-meter rotor diameter. The MOD-0 was not intended for commercial generation — it was a research platform, instrumented to measure loads, vibrations, and performance under real wind conditions. [26]

**MOD-0A** (1977): An upgraded 200 kW version. Multiple units were installed at test sites in Culebra (Puerto Rico), Clayton (New Mexico), and Oahu (Hawaii) — deliberately chosen to represent different wind regimes, climates, and grid conditions. [27]

**MOD-1** (1979): The programme's most ambitious and most troubled machine. Rated at 2 MW with two blades spanning 61 meters — a wingspan slightly larger than a Boeing 747's — the MOD-1 was installed on Howard's Knob near Boone, North Carolina. It generated infrasound that disturbed nearby residents, and its downwind rotor configuration caused severe structural loads as each blade passed through the tower's wind shadow. In its operational life, MOD-1 ran for only 330 hours out of a possible 29,112 and generated just 75,000 kWh out of a theoretical capacity of 43.7 million kWh. [28]

**MOD-2** (1980–1982): The programme's masterpiece. Designed and built by Boeing Engineering and Construction, the MOD-2 was rated at 2.5 MW with a 91-meter (300-foot) rotor — the largest wind turbine in the world at the time. Three units were installed in a cluster at **Goodnoe Hills**, Washington, overlooking the Columbia River Gorge, forming what was effectively the world's first utility-scale wind farm. The MOD-2 incorporated the lessons of every predecessor: an upwind rotor (avoiding tower shadow), partial-span pitch control, and a three-stage planetary gearbox built by Stal-Laval of Sweden. [29]

Meanwhile, in Denmark, a different approach was unfolding. In 1978, teachers and students at the Tvind school on the Jutland coast built a 2 MW wind turbine using community labour and open-source blade designs — the first "open-source" wind technology, decades before the term existed. The Tvind turbine is still operating. [30] In 1979, the Vestas company — a former blacksmith shop in the town of Lem — sold its first commercial wind turbine: a 30 kW machine with a 10-meter rotor. [31] These modest Danish machines, refined in the demanding winds of the North Sea coast, were about to change the world.

## 3.5 The California Wind Rush

While NASA engineers in white coats were testing megawatt-scale prototypes in the Columbia River Gorge, the private sector discovered something more powerful than technology: **tax policy**.

In 1978, the U.S. Congress passed the **Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA)**, which required electric utilities to purchase power from independent producers at their "avoided cost" — the price the utility would otherwise have paid to generate the electricity itself. For the first time, a private citizen could build a power plant and force the local utility to buy the output. [32]

That same year, the federal government introduced a **25 percent investment tax credit** for renewable energy projects. California, under Governor Jerry Brown, added its own state tax credits, effectively doubling the incentive. The combined effect was extraordinary: an investor could install a wind turbine, write off more than half the capital cost against taxes, and sell the electricity to the utility at a guaranteed price. The economics worked even if the turbine barely turned. [33]

The result was the **California Wind Rush**.

Between 1981 and 1987, developers installed approximately **17,000 wind turbines** across three mountain passes in southern and central California: Altamont Pass (east of San Francisco), Tehachapi Pass (between Bakersfield and the Mojave Desert), and San Gorgonio Pass (near Palm Springs). Altamont alone received over 6,200 turbines under twenty-six separate development projects, making it the largest concentration of wind turbines in the world. By 1985, California's wind installations produced roughly half of all wind-generated electricity on the planet. [34]

The machines were small by modern standards — typically 50 to 100 kW each, with rotor diameters of 15 to 20 meters. Many were American designs rushed to market to capture the tax credits before they expired. Many were unreliable. Some never worked at all. A significant fraction of installed turbines were not generating electricity and may have been erected primarily to harvest tax benefits. [35]

The machines that *did* work were overwhelmingly Danish.

Companies like **Vestas**, **Bonus** (later Siemens Wind Power), and **Nordtank** had spent the late 1970s refining the Danish concept — three blades, upwind, stall-regulated, asynchronous generator — in Denmark's demanding winds. When the California market opened, Danish manufacturers exported thousands of turbines to the American passes. They were simpler, more robust, and more reliable than the ambitious American designs. The Danish machines spun when the American machines stood idle. [36]

The **capacity factor** — the ratio of actual energy produced to the theoretical maximum — tells the story quantitatively:

$$
CF = \frac{E_{actual}}{P_{rated} \times T}
$$

where:
- $CF$ = capacity factor [dimensionless, typically expressed as a percentage]
- $E_{actual}$ = energy actually produced over the period [kWh]
- $P_{rated}$ = rated (nameplate) power of the turbine [kW]
- $T$ = total hours in the period [h] (8,760 hours for one year)

A well-sited Danish turbine in the California passes achieved capacity factors of 20 to 25 percent — modest by modern offshore standards (40–50 percent) but respectable for 1980s technology and those sites. Many of the American-built machines achieved capacity factors below 10 percent. Some achieved zero. [37]

The California Wind Rush ended as quickly as it began. The federal tax credits expired in 1985. Oil prices, which had spiked above $35 per barrel in 1981, collapsed to below $10 by 1986. California's generous avoided-cost payments were renegotiated downward. With the financial incentives gone, new wind development in the United States ground to a halt. It would not resume in earnest until the production tax credit was introduced in 1992. [38]

The rush left behind a complicated legacy. Thousands of abandoned or non-functional turbines littered the California passes — a landscape that critics of renewable energy would photograph for decades as evidence of wind power's failure. At Altamont Pass, studies conducted between 1998 and 2003 estimated that the turbines killed approximately 67 golden eagles, 188 red-tailed hawks, 348 American kestrels, and 440 burrowing owls per year — a bird mortality rate that remains among the highest of any wind installation in the world, and that catalysed the modern field of wind-wildlife interaction research. [39]

But the rush also proved something that no laboratory experiment could: wind energy, deployed at scale, could feed meaningful quantities of electricity into a utility grid. The technology worked. The economics, stripped of subsidy, did not — not yet. And the Danish manufacturers who had cut their teeth in California went home with market experience, manufacturing scale, and engineering confidence that would make Denmark the dominant force in global wind energy for the next two decades.

## 3.X Worked Example: From Gedser to a Modern Wind Farm — How Far Have We Come?

The capacity factor provides a single number that captures the combined effect of every improvement in wind turbine technology — better aerodynamics, taller towers, larger rotors, stronger sites. Let us trace that number from the Gedser turbine to a modern offshore wind farm.

**Step 1: Gedser turbine capacity factor (1957).**

The Gedser turbine had a rated power of 200 kW and produced an average of 220,000 kWh per year in winds averaging 6.3 m/s at hub height.

$$
CF_{Gedser} = \frac{E_{actual}}{P_{rated} \times 8{,}760} = \frac{220{,}000}{200 \times 8{,}760} = \frac{220{,}000}{1{,}752{,}000} = 0.126 = 12.6\%
$$

**Step 2: Modern 15 MW offshore turbine capacity factor.**

A modern 15 MW offshore wind turbine in the North Sea or Baltic Sea, with a 236-meter rotor and 150-meter hub height, typically achieves a capacity factor of approximately 45 percent — a reflection of taller towers reaching stronger and steadier winds, larger rotors sweeping more area, and advanced blade aerodynamics approaching the Betz limit.

$$
E_{modern} = 15{,}000 \times 8{,}760 \times 0.45 = 59{,}130{,}000 \text{ kWh/year} = 59.1 \text{ GWh/year}
$$

**Step 3: How many Gedser turbines to match one modern machine?**

$$
N_{Gedser} = \frac{59{,}130{,}000}{220{,}000} = 269 \text{ turbines}
$$

One modern 15 MW turbine produces as much electricity per year as 269 Gedser turbines would have.

**Step 4: Scale to a 500 MW offshore wind farm.**

A generic 500 MW offshore wind farm with 34 modern turbines produces:

$$
E_{farm} = 34 \times 59.1 = 2{,}009 \text{ GWh/year}
$$

The Gedser equivalent:

$$
N_{farm} = 34 \times 269 = 9{,}146 \text{ Gedser turbines}
$$

Nine thousand wind turbines. That number should sound familiar: it is almost exactly the number of windmills that operated in the Netherlands at the peak of the drainage era (Chapter 2). The parallel is not a coincidence — it illustrates how far the technology has advanced. A modern offshore wind farm replaces, in energy terms, the entire wind-powered infrastructure of a 17th-century nation — with thirty-four machines instead of nine thousand.

The improvement comes from three sources: better aerodynamics ($C_p$ increased from roughly 0.25 to 0.48), a vastly larger swept area (rotor diameter grew from 24 m to 236 m — nearly tenfold, meaning swept area grew a hundredfold), and stronger siting (offshore winds of 10+ m/s versus onshore 6.3 m/s, and because power scales with $v^3$, that alone accounts for a factor of $(10/6.3)^3 \approx 4.0$).

## Key Takeaways

- **Wind electricity began in 1887, but it took a scientist to make it matter.** Blyth lit his Scottish cottage; Brush powered his Cleveland mansion. But Poul la Cour — physicist, teacher, institution builder — turned the concept into a movement. His kratostat, his wind tunnel, his training school, and his Danish Wind Electricity Society laid the foundation for a century of Danish wind energy leadership.

- **Blade count decreased as the purpose changed from pumping to generating.** Multi-blade rotors (like Brush's 144-blade machine) produce high torque at low speed — ideal for pumping water. Electricity generation requires high rotational speed, which means fewer, slender, aerodynamic blades operating at high tip speed ratios ($\lambda \approx 6$–$8$). The Jacobs brothers' three-blade propeller anticipated the modern standard by half a century.

- **The Smith-Putnam turbine proved megawatt-scale wind power was feasible — and that blade fatigue would be the critical engineering challenge.** Its 1945 blade failure, caused by a repaired spar crack propagating under cyclic loading, foreshadowed the failure mode that would dominate wind turbine structural engineering for the next eighty years.

- **Government programmes advanced the technology but could not make it economical.** The Gedser turbine's Danish concept — three blades, upwind, stall-regulated, asynchronous generator — became the global standard. NASA's MOD-2 proved multi-megawatt operation was possible. Neither survived the withdrawal of public funding.

- **The California Wind Rush proved that tax policy can create an industry overnight — and destroy it just as fast.** The boom installed 17,000 turbines and demonstrated utility-scale wind power was real. The bust left abandoned machines and avian casualties. The Danish manufacturers who built reliable turbines amid the chaos emerged as the world's market leaders.

## For Further Reading

- **Putnam, P.C. (1948).** *Power from the Wind.* Van Nostrand Reinhold. The engineering monograph that documented the Smith-Putnam project from conception through blade failure. Still remarkably readable, with detailed aerodynamic and structural analysis. Available in university libraries and digital archives.

- **Manwell, J.F., McGowan, J.G., & Rogers, A.L. (2021).** *Wind Energy Explained: Theory, Design and Application,* 3rd ed. Wiley. Chapter 1 provides an excellent history of wind energy from antiquity to the present, with technical depth on each era. The standard graduate textbook for wind energy engineering.

- **Righter, R.W. (1996).** *Wind Energy in America: A History.* University of Oklahoma Press. The definitive history of American wind power, from water-pumping windmills on the Great Plains to the California Wind Rush. Righter covers the political, economic, and social dimensions that engineering histories often omit — essential for understanding why good technology does not always win.
