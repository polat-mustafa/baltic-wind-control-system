# Chapter 15: Installation: Vessels, Weather Windows, and the Art of Building at Sea

*The weather broke on Tuesday.*

*Three days of grey swells and horizontal rain had kept every vessel on the construction site idle — three days of the cable lay vessel riding on its dynamic positioning thrusters, burning fuel but laying nothing, three days of the jack-up vessel standing on its legs with its crane folded and secured, three days of a quarter-million-euro daily burn rate producing precisely zero installed components. Kaan had spent those days on the SOV, reviewing cable routing plans with Nora and watching the wave forecast on his tablet as each six-hour ECMWF update showed the low-pressure system inching eastward across the Baltic.*

*Now the sea was flat. Significant wave height had dropped below one metre overnight, the wind had backed to the south-west and fallen to eight knots, and the construction site had come alive. A CTV was already running technicians to the completed foundations. The rock placement vessel was back on station, dumping graded stone onto a cable crossing. And the jack-up vessel — the vessel he had been watching from a distance for two weeks — had sent a fast rescue craft to collect him.*

*Marc Janssens was waiting at the top of the accommodation ladder when Kaan climbed aboard. The installation campaign manager was a compact Belgian in his late forties, with a shaved head, a hi-vis vest covered in radio clips and cable ties, and a worn A5 notebook tucked into his chest pocket. He shook Kaan's hand with the grip of someone who had spent twenty years climbing ladders on offshore platforms — first in the North Sea oil fields, then on the wind farms that were replacing them.*

*"Welcome aboard," Marc said, already walking. "We have a five-day window. Five turbines if everything goes perfectly. Four if the blade crane needs a re-certification lift. Three if the forecast is wrong." He pulled the notebook from his pocket and showed Kaan a page ruled into columns: date, significant wave height, wind speed, operation, status. The first fourteen days of the campaign were recorded in neat blue ink. Seven of them were marked in red: weather downtime. "The sea decides the schedule," Marc said, snapping the notebook shut. "We just try to be ready when it says yes."*

*He led Kaan to the bridge deck, where a panoramic window overlooked the crane boom and the forward deck. Below them, a turbine tower section — a tapered steel cylinder thirty metres long and painted in fresh white primer — sat in a transport frame, waiting to be lifted. The crane operator was running pre-lift checks. In three hours, that tower section would be standing on a monopile foundation two hundred metres away. In two days, if the weather held, a nacelle heavier than a loaded Boeing 747 would sit on top of it. And in three days, three blades longer than a football pitch would be bolted to the hub, and the turbine would be complete — ready for electrical connection, commissioning, and its first rotation.*

*"Let me show you how we build a wind farm," Marc said.*

## 15.1 The Floating Crane That Learned to Stand

The first offshore wind farm was installed with equipment borrowed from a different century of marine engineering.

In 1991, when Elkraft and SEAS erected eleven Bonus B35/450 turbines in the shallow waters off Vindeby, Denmark, there was no such thing as a wind turbine installation vessel. The developers used conventional flat-top barges — the same type of vessel used to transport cargo in harbours and rivers — fitted with a mobile crane. The barges were towed to each foundation position by a tug. The turbines, each rated at 450 kW with a hub height of 38 metres and a rotor diameter of 35 metres, were small enough that a standard mobile crane could lift the nacelle and blades. The eleven turbines were erected in eleven days, limited to a maximum of two installations per 24-hour period in calm seas because the barges — unlike jack-up vessels — had no way to lift themselves out of the water, and every wave that passed beneath the hull translated directly into motion at the crane tip. [1]

The barge-and-crane approach worked for turbines that weighed a few dozen tonnes and stood 40 metres tall. It could not work for turbines that weighed hundreds of tonnes and stood 150 metres tall. The offshore wind industry needed a vessel that could carry multiple turbine sets across open water, position itself over a foundation with centimetre accuracy, lift itself above the waves to create a stable platform, and then hoist components to heights that exceeded the reach of any conventional marine crane.

The answer was the **wind turbine installation vessel** (WTIV) — a self-propelled jack-up vessel with a heavy-lift crane, designed specifically for the repetitive cycle of transit, jack-up, lift, jack-down, and transit that defines offshore wind construction.

The world's first purpose-built WTIV was the **MPI Resolution**, ordered in 2002 by Mayflower Energy Ltd and built at the Shanhaiguan shipyard in Qinhuangdao, China, to a design by the Danish naval architects Knud E. Hansen. The vessel was originally budgeted at £20 million; by the time it was delivered — late, over budget, and with significant modifications — the final cost had reached £53 million. Mayflower Energy went into administration with debts of £17.7 million, and the vessel was sold to a group of its own managers, backed by the Japanese bank Mizuho International, who formed MPI Offshore. Despite its troubled birth, MPI Resolution went on to install turbines at North Hoyle, Scroby Sands, Barrow, and Lynn and Inner Dowsing — some of the earliest commercial offshore wind farms in British waters. Its crane could lift approximately 300 tonnes, and its four legs allowed it to operate in water depths up to about 30 metres. [2]

Two decades later, the vessels that install offshore wind turbines are among the largest and most specialised ships afloat. The scale escalation mirrors the turbines themselves:

| Vessel | Year | Crane Capacity | Leg Length | Deck Area | Lifting Height |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MPI Resolution | 2003 | ~300 t | ~70 m | ~2,500 m² | ~80 m |
| Voltaire (Jan De Nul) | 2022 | 3,000+ t | 130 m | ~5,600 m² | ~165 m |
| Charybdis (Dominion/DEME) | 2025 | 2,200 t | 130 m | ~5,400 m² | ~155 m |
| Orion (DEME) | 2022 | 5,000 t | DP3 (no legs) | 8,000 m² | 178 m |

The **Voltaire**, delivered to Belgian contractor Jan De Nul in 2022, represents the current generation of jack-up WTIVs. At 179.5 metres long and 60 metres wide, with four legs each 130 metres long, the vessel can operate in water depths up to 80 metres and carry a payload of 14,000 tonnes — enough for several complete turbine sets (tower, nacelle, and three blades). Its crane, supplied by Huisman, lifts over 3,000 tonnes to heights exceeding 165 metres above deck. When the Voltaire stands on its legs, the tips of those legs extend higher than the Eiffel Tower. [3]

The **Orion**, delivered to Belgian contractor DEME in 2022, takes a different approach entirely. At 216.5 metres long with a 5,000-tonne Liebherr crane capable of reaching 178 metres, the Orion is not a jack-up vessel — it is a dynamically positioned (DP3) crane vessel, kept in position by thrusters rather than legs on the seabed. This makes it faster to reposition between foundation locations (no jacking cycle) but more sensitive to wave conditions during lifting. The Orion's primary role is installing the heaviest components — XXL monopiles exceeding 2,000 tonnes and jacket foundations — rather than the repetitive turbine-by-turbine installation cycle that jack-up WTIVs perform. [4]

The **Charybdis**, built by Seatrium AmFELS in Brownsville, Texas, and delivered in 2025, holds a distinction that has nothing to do with its crane capacity. It is the first WTIV built in the United States and the first to comply with the **Jones Act** — the Merchant Marine Act of 1920, which requires that vessels transporting goods between US ports be built in America, owned by Americans, and crewed by Americans. For years, the absence of a Jones Act-compliant WTIV forced US offshore wind developers to use European vessels under complex workarounds — European WTIVs could install turbines (classified as "construction" rather than "transportation") but could not carry turbine components from a US port to an installation site. The Charybdis, with its 2,200-tonne crane and 130-metre legs, eliminates this bottleneck for the 2.6 GW Coastal Virginia Offshore Wind project — 176 turbines, the largest offshore wind farm planned in the United States. [5]

## 15.2 Standing on the Seabed

A jack-up WTIV's most important operation happens before the crane lifts anything. It is the act of standing up.

Each of the vessel's legs — typically four, sometimes six, each a lattice or tubular steel structure 100 to 130 metres long — terminates in a **spudcan**: a flat, circular steel footing 10 to 15 metres in diameter, shaped like an inverted cone, that distributes the vessel's weight across the seabed. The jacking system, powered by hydraulic or rack-and-pinion drives, pushes the legs downward through the hull until the spudcans contact the seabed, then continues to push until the hull lifts clear of the water. The entire process — from floating vessel to elevated platform — takes approximately two to four hours, depending on water depth and soil conditions. [6]

But contact with the seabed is not enough. The vessel must prove that the seabed can support it.

### Preloading

Before any crane operation begins, the vessel performs a **preload test** — a procedure borrowed directly from the oil and gas jack-up industry and governed by the same engineering principles. The purpose is to compress the soil beneath each spudcan to a bearing capacity that exceeds the maximum load the vessel will experience during the most severe storm forecast for the operational period.

The procedure is methodical. The vessel pumps seawater into ballast tanks to increase its weight, applying this load to the legs in diagonal pairs. Each pair is loaded to the preload target — typically 1.25 to 1.50 times the maximum storm reaction — while the crew monitors leg penetration with millimetre precision. If the soil yields, the leg sinks further. The cycle repeats until the seabed ceases to compress and the legs stabilise. Only then is the preload ballast discharged and the hull raised to its operational air gap. [7]

The required preload is determined by a bearing capacity analysis that considers the soil profile beneath each spudcan:

$$
P_{\text{preload}} \geq \gamma_f \times R_{\text{storm,max}}
$$

where:
- $P_{\text{preload}}$ = preload per leg [kN]
- $\gamma_f$ = safety factor (typically 1.25–1.50, per DNV-ST-N001) [dimensionless]
- $R_{\text{storm,max}}$ = maximum vertical reaction on the most heavily loaded leg during the design storm event [kN]

### Air Gap

Once preloaded, the hull is raised until its underside clears the highest wave crest that could occur during operations — the **air gap**. The minimum air gap is calculated as:

$$
h_{\text{air}} = \eta_{\text{max}} + \Delta h_{\text{tide}} + \delta_{\text{set}} + s
$$

where:
- $h_{\text{air}}$ = clearance between the underside of the hull and mean sea level [m]
- $\eta_{\text{max}}$ = maximum wave crest elevation for the operational sea state [m]
- $\Delta h_{\text{tide}}$ = tidal range (highest astronomical tide minus chart datum) [m]
- $\delta_{\text{set}}$ = allowance for settlement and seabed scour during operations [m]
- $s$ = safety margin (typically 1.0–1.5 m per DNV-ST-N001) [m]

In the southern Baltic, where tidal ranges are negligible (typically less than 0.3 m) but storm surges can add 1.0 to 1.5 m, the air gap calculation is dominated by wave crest elevation and surge. A typical operational air gap for a WTIV in 30 metres of water is 12 to 15 metres — enough to clear even the most severe waves likely to arrive during the forecast period. [8]

### Punch-Through

The most feared hazard in jack-up operations is **punch-through** — a sudden, uncontrolled leg penetration that occurs when a spudcan breaks through a hard soil layer into a weaker layer beneath. The effect is analogous to stepping onto a frozen puddle: the surface holds for a moment, then gives way. A punch-through can cause the hull to tilt violently, shift laterally, and — in extreme cases — capsize. The risk is highest in layered soils: stiff clay over soft clay, sand over soft clay, or a cemented crust over loose sediment.

In the Polish Baltic, where the seabed comprises glacial deposits from the Weichselian glaciation — stiff clay interbedded with sand lenses, underlain by glacial till — the geotechnical survey conducted before the WTIV arrives at each location is not a formality. It is the difference between a controlled two-hour jacking operation and an emergency. Site-specific soil borings, cone penetration tests, and bearing capacity analyses are performed for every jack-up position on the wind farm site. [9]

## 15.3 The Construction Ballet

Building a 500 MW offshore wind farm is not a single construction project. It is four or five overlapping campaigns, each requiring different vessels, different crews, different weather windows, and different supply chains — all choreographed across the same patch of sea over a period of two to three years.

The sequence is not arbitrary. Each campaign depends on the one before it, and the order is dictated by a simple rule: **you cannot install what you cannot connect, and you cannot connect what you cannot stand on.**

### Phase 1: Export Cable (Months 1–4)

The export cables are laid first, because they require the longest uninterrupted weather windows and because they must be in place before the offshore substation can be energised. The cable lay vessel installs the two 220 kV circuits from the landfall to the OSS location, burying them in the seabed as described in Chapter 14. The landfall HDD bore is drilled from onshore during this phase. Duration: three to four months, including mobilisation, lay, burial, and post-lay survey.

### Phase 2: Foundations (Months 3–14)

Foundation installation begins as soon as the first monopile can be driven without interfering with the export cable route. A heavy-lift vessel — either a jack-up WTIV with a high-capacity crane or a DP crane vessel like the Orion — transports monopiles from the marshalling port and drives them into the seabed using a hydraulic impact hammer, as described in Chapter 13. Each monopile takes approximately two to three days from arrival to completion (including upending, hammering, and transition piece installation). For 34 foundations, the campaign lasts eight to twelve months, with weather delays adding 30 to 50 percent to the theoretical minimum duration.

### Phase 3: Array Cables (Months 8–16)

Array cable installation begins after a sufficient number of foundations are in place, typically once the first string of five or six turbines has all its foundations and J-tubes installed. A smaller cable lay vessel installs the 66 kV array cables, pulling each end into the J-tubes of adjacent foundations and burying the cable between them. The array cables must be in place before the turbines they connect can be commissioned — but they can be installed in parallel with ongoing foundation work elsewhere on the site.

### Phase 4: Turbine Installation (Months 12–24)

The turbines are installed last, because they are the most weather-sensitive operation and because every turbine requires a completed foundation with its array cable pulled in and tested before installation begins. The WTIV loads a set of turbine components at the marshalling port — typically two to three complete turbine sets per voyage (tower sections, nacelles, blade sets) — transits to the site, jacks up at the first foundation, and begins the lift sequence.

### Phase 5: Commissioning (Months 18–30)

Commissioning overlaps with the final phase of turbine installation. As each turbine is mechanically complete, an electrical team connects the array cable termination, tests the switchgear, and prepares the turbine for energisation. The commissioning sequence — from individual turbine testing to string energisation to full farm export — is the subject of Part IX (Chapters 38–42).

The overlap between phases is critical. At the peak of construction activity, five to eight vessels may be operating simultaneously within the wind farm site: a jack-up WTIV installing turbines, a heavy-lift vessel driving monopiles, a cable lay vessel installing array cables, a rock placement vessel protecting cable crossings, an SOV supporting technicians, CTVs running crew transfers, and a guard vessel monitoring the construction exclusion zone. Coordinating these vessels — each with different weather limits, different transit speeds, and different port requirements — is the installation campaign manager's central challenge. [11]

### Marshalling

The **marshalling port** is the onshore staging area where turbine components are received from their factories, stored, pre-assembled if required, and loaded onto the WTIV for transit to site. The port requirements are demanding:

- **Quay load bearing:** 15 to 25 tonnes per square metre, to support the concentrated weight of tower sections, nacelles, and blade racks
- **Water depth alongside:** 8 to 12 metres minimum, to accommodate the WTIV's draft and leg clearance
- **Storage area:** 20 to 50 hectares, to hold components for 30 to 50 turbines simultaneously (blades alone are 115 metres long and require specialist racks)
- **Quay length:** 300 to 500 metres of continuous heavy-lift quay
- **Access:** Road and rail connections for component delivery from factories across Europe

In Europe, the leading marshalling ports for offshore wind include Esbjerg (Denmark), which has handled more offshore wind turbine components than any other port in the world; Cuxhaven (Germany), which is investing EUR 300 million in three new berths totalling 1,250 metres of quay; and in the Polish Baltic, the Port of Gdańsk, where the new T5 installation terminal — 21 hectares of reinforced quay — is being developed specifically for the country's first generation of offshore wind projects. [12]

## 15.4 Weather Windows

Every offshore operation has a **weather limit** — a maximum significant wave height ($H_s$) and maximum wind speed above which the operation must stop. These limits are not suggestions. They are hard constraints, verified by the marine warranty surveyor and enforced by the vessel master, because exceeding them risks damage to equipment, injury to personnel, or — in extreme cases — loss of the vessel.

The limits vary by operation and by vessel:

| Operation | Max $H_s$ [m] | Max Wind [m/s] | Typical Duration [h] |
|---|---|---|---|
| WTIV transit (floating) | 2.0–3.0 | 20 | 4–12 |
| Jack-up leg lowering | 1.2–1.5 | 15 | 2–3 |
| Jack-up preloading | 2.0 (jacked up) | 20 | 4–8 |
| Tower section lift | 1.5–2.0 (jacked up) | 12–15 | 3–5 |
| Nacelle lift | 1.5–2.0 (jacked up) | 10–12 | 4–6 |
| **Blade lift** | **1.5** | **10** | **2–4** |
| Cable lay and burial | 1.5–2.0 | 15 | continuous |
| CTV crew transfer | 1.5 | 15 | 0.5 |
| SOV gangway transfer | 2.5–3.0 | 20 | 0.5 |
| Monopile hammering (jacked up) | 2.0 | 20 | 4–8 |

The blade lift is the critical constraint. A single blade for a 15 MW turbine is 115.5 metres long, weighs approximately 65 tonnes, and presents a sail area of over 200 square metres to the wind. Even at moderate wind speeds, the aerodynamic force on a blade suspended from a crane is enormous — and the blade must be rotated from horizontal (as stored on the deck) to vertical (for bolting to the hub), passing through every angle of wind attack in between. This is why blade lifts are limited to wind speeds below 10 m/s (approximately 19 knots) — the most restrictive weather criterion in the entire turbine installation sequence. [13]

The consequence is arithmetic. If the blade lift limit is $H_s \leq 1.5$ m and wind speed $\leq 10$ m/s, the installation campaign can only proceed during hours when both conditions are simultaneously satisfied. The fraction of time that meets these criteria — the **weather window availability** — depends on the site, the season, and the year:

$$
W = P(H_s \leq h_{\text{lim}}) \times P(V_w \leq v_{\text{lim}})
$$

where:
- $W$ = weather window availability [fraction of time, 0 to 1]
- $P(H_s \leq h_{\text{lim}})$ = probability that significant wave height is at or below the operational limit
- $P(V_w \leq v_{\text{lim}})$ = probability that wind speed is at or below the operational limit

This assumes independence between wave height and wind speed, which is approximately true for short-term variations but not for weather systems. In practice, high waves and high winds arrive together and depart together, making weather windows more clustered (and more predictable) than a pure probability model would suggest. [14]

In the southern Baltic Sea, the seasonal pattern is pronounced:

| Month | Mean $H_s$ [m] | $P(H_s \leq 1.5\text{ m})$ | $P(V_w \leq 10\text{ m/s})$ | Est. $W$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 1.4 | 55% | 45% | ~25% |
| April | 0.8 | 80% | 65% | ~52% |
| June | 0.6 | 90% | 75% | ~68% |
| August | 0.7 | 87% | 72% | ~63% |
| October | 1.0 | 72% | 58% | ~42% |
| December | 1.3 | 60% | 48% | ~29% |

The installation season runs from approximately **April to October**, when weather window availability exceeds 40 to 50 percent. During the winter months (November to March), the combination of higher waves, stronger winds, shorter daylight hours, and the risk of sea ice in the eastern Baltic makes turbine installation impractical. A vessel that can install one turbine every two to three days in summer may manage one every five to seven days in the shoulder months — and none at all in January.

This seasonality is the reason offshore wind farm construction typically spans two to three installation seasons. A 34-turbine farm cannot be completed in a single summer, even with the most capable vessel. The weather window analysis — performed months before the first vessel arrives — determines the number of installation seasons required, the number of turbines targeted per season, and the contractual penalties that apply if delays push completion into the next year. [15]

### Forecasting

The installation campaign manager's most important tool is not the crane. It is the weather forecast.

Modern offshore wind construction relies on **deterministic and ensemble numerical weather prediction** (NWP) models — principally the ECMWF's Integrated Forecasting System (IFS), supplemented by regional models (HIRLAM, COSMO, WRF) and site-specific hindcast data. The forecast is updated every six hours (00, 06, 12, 18 UTC) and provides predictions of significant wave height, peak period, wind speed, wind direction, visibility, and air temperature for the next 10 to 15 days. For operational decisions — "Do we start a 24-hour lift sequence tonight?" — the forecast horizon that matters is 24 to 72 hours, where the ECMWF's skill in predicting $H_s$ in European waters has a root-mean-square error of approximately 0.2 to 0.3 m. [16]

The decision framework is conservative. An operation that requires 24 hours of continuous weather below the limit will only be started if the forecast shows at least 36 hours of compliant conditions — the additional 12 hours providing a contingency buffer for forecast error and for safe completion if conditions deteriorate faster than predicted. This **alpha factor** (the ratio of forecast window to operation duration) is specified in DNV-ST-N001 and typically ranges from 1.25 to 1.50 for planned operations.

## 15.5 Tower, Nacelle, Blade

The turbine installation sequence at each foundation position follows a procedure that has been refined over thousands of repetitions across hundreds of offshore wind farms. The steps are invariant; the timing depends on the weather, the vessel, and the turbine type.

### Step 1: Jack-up and position (2–4 hours)

The WTIV transits from the marshalling port or from the previous foundation, floating on its hull at 8 to 10 knots. On arrival, it positions itself using GPS and its dynamic positioning system, aligning the crane boom with the monopile foundation. The legs are lowered. The spudcans contact the seabed. The jacking system raises the hull to its operational air gap. Preloading is performed if the location has not been previously verified. The vessel is now a stable platform, independent of the sea state beneath it.

### Step 2: Tower installation (3–6 hours)

A 15 MW turbine tower is typically delivered in two or three sections, each 25 to 35 metres long and weighing 200 to 400 tonnes. The crane lifts the first (bottom) tower section from the deck, swings it over the side, and lowers it onto the transition piece bolted to the monopile. A crew of bolting technicians — working at height on the transition piece — guides the tower section into alignment using tag lines and hydraulic alignment tools. The base flange is bolted with 120 to 180 high-strength bolts (M48 or M56, torqued to specification). The second tower section is lifted and bolted to the first. The complete tower stands 100 to 115 metres above sea level.

### Step 3: Nacelle installation (4–6 hours)

The nacelle — the enclosure containing the generator, gearbox, main bearing, yaw system, and control electronics — weighs 500 to 650 tonnes for a 15 MW turbine. It is the single heaviest lift of the installation sequence. The crane lifts the nacelle from its transport frame on deck, raises it to the top of the tower (now 100 to 115 metres above the water), and lowers it onto the tower-top flange. The yaw bearing interface must be aligned to within millimetres. Hydraulic jacks on the nacelle bed frame make fine adjustments. The nacelle is bolted to the tower with another ring of high-strength bolts.

The nacelle lift is the moment that defines the crane's minimum capacity. A crane rated at 2,200 tonnes at 30-metre radius will have a significantly lower capacity at the 40 to 50-metre radius required to swing the nacelle clear of the tower and over the foundation. The relationship between capacity and radius follows the fundamental law of moments:

$$
M_{\text{crane}} = W \times R
$$

where:
- $M_{\text{crane}}$ = crane moment (approximately constant for a given boom configuration) [kN·m]
- $W$ = weight of the lifted load [kN]
- $R$ = radius from crane centre to load [m]

A crane rated at 3,000 tonnes at 30 m radius can lift only 1,500 tonnes at 60 m radius — and for turbines with rotor diameters exceeding 200 metres, the required radius during nacelle installation is pushing the limits of even the largest cranes afloat. [17]

### Step 4: Blade installation (2–4 hours per blade, 6–12 hours total)

The three blades are lifted individually, each weighing 55 to 70 tonnes and measuring 115 metres in length. The blade is stored horizontally on the deck in a blade rack. A yoke or blade clamp grips the blade at its root and midspan. The crane lifts the blade to vertical, rotates it, and aligns the root with one of the three hub bolt patterns. A team of technicians inside the hub guides the root flange into position using cameras and tag lines. The root is secured with approximately 72 bolts per blade (M36 or M42, fully torqued).

The blade lift is the most weather-sensitive operation. The blade's enormous length and surface area create significant wind loads — a crosswind of 10 m/s on a blade suspended at 150 metres generates forces of several tonnes, requiring the crane to compensate continuously. Advanced blade-lifting tools include **tuned mass dampers** and **active winch damping systems** that reduce pendulum oscillations during the lift. Even with these aids, the blade installation window is the narrowest of all operations: $H_s \leq 1.5$ m and wind speed $\leq 10$ m/s, with gusts below 12 to 14 m/s. [18]

### Step 5: Jack-down and transit (2–3 hours)

Once all three blades are installed, the WTIV lowers its hull back to the water, retracts its legs, and transits to the next foundation — or returns to the marshalling port to reload the next set of turbine components.

The complete cycle — jack-up, tower, nacelle, three blades, jack-down — takes **24 to 48 hours** of favourable weather per turbine. The fastest installation vessels, operating in ideal conditions, have achieved rates of **one turbine per day**. Averaged over an entire campaign including weather delays, transit, and port loading, the realistic rate is **one turbine every three to five days**. [19]

## 15.6 The Vessel Spread

The fleet of vessels required to construct an offshore wind farm — the **vessel spread** — represents the single largest category of installation expenditure. Unlike turbines or foundations, which are purchased once, vessels are chartered by the day, and every day of weather delay is a day of cost without progress.

A representative vessel spread for a 500 MW offshore wind farm includes:

| Vessel Type | Typical Day Rate [EUR] | Campaign Duration | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| WTIV (turbine installation) | 200,000–350,000 | 6–10 months | Tower, nacelle, blade lifts |
| Heavy-lift vessel (foundations) | 150,000–300,000 | 8–12 months | Monopile driving, TP installation |
| Cable lay vessel (export) | 150,000–250,000 | 3–4 months | Export cable installation |
| Cable lay vessel (array) | 100,000–200,000 | 4–6 months | Inter-array cable installation |
| SOV (service operations vessel) | 30,000–50,000 | 12–24 months | Technician accommodation, transfers |
| Rock placement vessel | 50,000–80,000 | 2–3 months | Scour protection, cable crossings |
| Guard vessel | 5,000–10,000 | 24–30 months | Exclusion zone enforcement |
| CTVs (crew transfer, 2–4 units) | 3,000–5,000 each | 12–24 months | Daily crew movements |

The total vessel spread cost is:

$$
C_{\text{spread}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i \times D_i
$$

where:
- $C_{\text{spread}}$ = total vessel charter cost [EUR]
- $r_i$ = daily charter rate for vessel $i$ [EUR/day]
- $D_i$ = charter duration for vessel $i$ [days]
- $n$ = number of vessels in the spread

For a typical 500 MW project, the vessel spread cost — excluding fuel, port fees, and personnel — ranges from EUR 200 to 400 million, representing 13 to 27 percent of total CAPEX. The WTIV alone accounts for 40 to 50 percent of the total vessel cost. [20]

The cost of a single day of weather delay depends on how many vessels are idle:

$$
C_{\text{delay}} = \sum_{i \in \text{idle}} r_i
$$

When the WTIV is waiting for weather, the SOV, the guard vessel, and possibly the cable lay vessel are also waiting. On a day when all major vessels are idle, the combined day rate can exceed EUR 500,000 — half a million euros for zero progress. Over a 10-month turbine installation campaign with 35 percent weather downtime, the cost of waiting is approximately EUR 50 to 70 million. This is not a penalty; it is the expected cost of building in the sea.

The global fleet of WTIVs capable of installing 15 MW-class turbines comprises approximately 10 to 15 vessels as of 2025. The demand for these vessels — driven by the simultaneous construction of wind farms in the North Sea, the Baltic, the Irish Sea, the US East Coast, and the Taiwan Strait — exceeds supply. WTIV day rates have risen significantly since 2020, and projects that fail to secure a vessel charter two to three years in advance risk delays of an entire construction season. The vessel availability constraint is, alongside consenting and grid connection, one of the three principal bottlenecks in the global offshore wind supply chain. [21]

## 15.7 Worked Example: Installing 34 Turbines

Consider the turbine installation campaign for a generic 500 MW offshore wind farm: 34 turbines rated at 15 MW each, located 30 km from the marshalling port in 25 to 35 metres of water.

### Step 1: Vessel capacity and cycle time

The WTIV carries **two complete turbine sets per voyage** (limited by deck space: 2 × tower sections + 2 nacelles + 6 blades). Each voyage comprises:

| Activity | Duration |
|---|---|
| Port loading | 12 h |
| Transit to site (30 km at 8 knots) | 4 h |
| Jack-up + preload (first position) | 4 h |
| Turbine 1: tower + nacelle + 3 blades | 30 h |
| Jack-down + transit to next position | 3 h |
| Jack-up (second position, pre-verified) | 2 h |
| Turbine 2: tower + nacelle + 3 blades | 30 h |
| Jack-down + transit to port | 4 h |
| **Total per voyage (2 turbines)** | **89 h ≈ 3.7 days** |

In calm conditions, the vessel can complete **2 turbines every 4 days** — an effective rate of 0.5 turbines per day.

### Step 2: Weather window impact

The installation campaign runs from April to October (214 days). Using the seasonal weather window availability from Section 15.4:

$$
D_{\text{productive}} = \sum_{\text{months}} d_m \times W_m
$$

| Month | Days ($d_m$) | Weather Availability ($W_m$) | Productive Days |
|---|---|---|---|
| April | 30 | 0.52 | 15.6 |
| May | 31 | 0.60 | 18.6 |
| June | 30 | 0.68 | 20.4 |
| July | 31 | 0.65 | 20.2 |
| August | 31 | 0.63 | 19.5 |
| September | 30 | 0.50 | 15.0 |
| October | 31 | 0.42 | 13.0 |
| **Total** | **214** | — | **122.3** |

Of 214 calendar days, approximately **122 days** are productive — a seasonal availability of 57%.

### Step 3: Installation capacity per season

At 2 turbines per 4 productive days:

$$
N_{\text{season}} = \frac{D_{\text{productive}}}{D_{\text{cycle}} / 2} = \frac{122.3}{4 / 2} = \frac{122.3}{2} = 61 \text{ turbines (theoretical)}
$$

But this theoretical capacity is reduced by:
- Mechanical downtime (crane maintenance, vessel systems): 5%
- Port delays (component delivery, quay congestion): 5%
- Operational contingency (unforeseen events): 5%

Effective capacity: $61 \times 0.85 = 52$ turbines per season.

Since 34 turbines is well within the single-season capacity of 52, the campaign can be completed in **one installation season** — approximately April to September, with October as contingency.

### Step 4: Campaign cost

$$
C_{\text{WTIV}} = r_{\text{WTIV}} \times D_{\text{campaign}} = 275{,}000 \times 180 = \text{EUR } 49.5\text{M}
$$

where 180 days is the actual campaign duration (April to September inclusive, with mobilisation). Adding support vessels:

| Vessel | Day Rate [EUR] | Days | Cost [EUR M] |
|---|---|---|---|
| WTIV | 275,000 | 180 | 49.5 |
| SOV | 40,000 | 180 | 7.2 |
| Guard vessel | 8,000 | 200 | 1.6 |
| CTVs (×3) | 4,000 × 3 | 180 | 2.2 |
| **Turbine installation spread** | — | — | **60.5** |

This excludes the foundation installation spread (separate campaign) and the cable lay vessel spread (covered in Chapter 14).

### Step 5: Cost of weather delays

The total weather downtime is $180 - 122 = 58$ days. On each idle day, the vessel spread still incurs:

$$
C_{\text{weather}} = 58 \times (275{,}000 + 40{,}000 + 8{,}000 + 12{,}000) = 58 \times 335{,}000 = \text{EUR } 19.4\text{M}
$$

**EUR 19.4 million** — the cost of the sea saying no. This represents 32 percent of the turbine installation spread cost, and it cannot be avoided — only anticipated and budgeted. An installation campaign manager who delivers 34 turbines in one season, within budget, including the weather delays, has done the job correctly. The weather is not a failure; it is a parameter.

### Step 6: Total installation cost (all campaigns)

| Campaign | Estimated Cost [EUR M] |
|---|---|
| Foundation installation (spread + hammer + TP) | 80–100 |
| Cable installation (export + array) | 55 (from Ch 14) |
| Turbine installation (spread + logistics) | 60.5 |
| Commissioning support vessels | 10–15 |
| Port fees, fuel, marine coordination | 15–20 |
| **Total installation** | **220–250** |

As a percentage of total CAPEX (EUR 1,500M): approximately **15–17%**. Combined with foundation cost (EUR 316M = 21%, from Chapter 13) and cable cost (EUR 145M = 10%, from Chapter 14), the entire "Building in the Sea" scope — Part IV of this book — represents approximately **45–48% of a 500 MW offshore wind farm's capital cost**.

The turbines generate the electricity. The grid exports it. The SCADA system controls it. But nearly half the money is spent putting steel in the seabed, cable on the seafloor, and blades in the air.

## Key Takeaways

- **A wind turbine installation vessel (WTIV) is a self-propelled jack-up vessel with a heavy-lift crane,** evolved from simple barges at Vindeby (1991) to 180-metre ships with 5,000-tonne cranes and 130-metre legs. The global fleet of WTIVs capable of installing 15 MW turbines numbers only 10 to 15 vessels — a critical bottleneck in the supply chain.

- **The blade lift is the weather constraint that governs the installation season.** A 115-metre blade suspended at 150 metres height is limited to wind speeds below 10 m/s and significant wave heights below 1.5 m — the most restrictive criteria in offshore construction. In the southern Baltic, these conditions are met approximately 57% of the time between April and October, and less than 30% in winter.

- **Offshore wind farm construction is a 30-month choreography of five overlapping campaigns** — export cables, foundations, array cables, turbines, and commissioning — coordinated across five to eight vessels with a combined daily cost exceeding EUR 500,000. The construction sequence is dictated by dependencies: you cannot install what you cannot connect, and you cannot connect what you cannot stand on.

- **Weather downtime is not a failure — it is a parameter.** A 180-day turbine installation campaign loses approximately 58 days to weather, costing EUR 19 million in idle vessel charges. This cost is unavoidable, predictable, and budgeted — the price of building in the sea.

- **Part IV in perspective: foundations, cables, and installation together account for nearly half of total CAPEX** (EUR 680–710 million of EUR 1,500 million). The turbines are the technology; the sea is the cost.

*The thirty-fourth blade was bolted to its hub at seventeen minutes past three on a September afternoon.*

*Kaan stood on the bridge deck of the WTIV beside Marc, watching through the panoramic window as the bolting crew in the hub sent the "torque complete" signal over the radio. The crane operator slackened the blade yoke, and the blade — one hundred and fifteen metres of carbon and glass fibre, painted white with a red tip — hung free for a moment, balanced on its root bolts, before the yoke was swung clear and lowered to the deck. The rotor was complete. The turbine was complete. The wind farm was complete.*

*Not complete in the electrical sense — the commissioning teams would spend months connecting, testing, and energising each turbine and string. But mechanically, structurally, every piece of steel and copper and composite that needed to be in the sea was in the sea. Thirty-four monopiles driven into glacial clay. Thirty-four transition pieces grouted and bolted. One hundred and fifty kilometres of submarine cable buried in the sand. Two offshore substations standing on their jacket foundations. And now thirty-four nacelles, each heavier than a loaded Boeing 747, sitting on their towers above the Baltic, with one hundred and two blades bolted to their hubs, each blade longer than a football pitch.*

*Marc closed his notebook — the last page of the campaign. Kaan had seen him fill it, day by day: two hundred and six days from the first jacking operation to the last blade bolt. Sixty-three of those days marked in red. Weather. The sea saying no. But a hundred and forty-three days of lifts, transits, jacks, and bolts — a hundred and forty-three days of saying yes.*

*"Thirty-four turbines," Marc said, capping his pen. "Thirty-four jack-up cycles. One hundred and two blade lifts. Zero lost-time incidents." He looked at the line of turbines stretching north toward the horizon, their rotors locked in the installation position — three blades forming a Y, waiting for the signal to turn. "That is a good campaign."*

*The WTIV would be gone by the end of the week, jacking down for the last time and sailing to its next charter — a project in the German Bight, then one in the Irish Sea. The SOV would stay, along with the technicians and the commissioning engineers. The construction phase was ending. The operational phase was about to begin.*

*But before any turbine could turn, before any electron could flow from generator to grid, the offshore substation had to be energised. Two hundred and twenty thousand volts would course through the export cable to shore, stepping up through transformers, passing through switchgear, metered and monitored and controlled by systems that Kaan had not yet seen from the inside. The substation was the heart of the electrical system — and he had not yet set foot inside it.*

*"Next week," Anders said over the radio from the SOV. He had been watching the final blade lift from the observation deck, binoculars in hand. "Next week, we go inside the substation. And before we touch anything in there, you are going to understand alternating current. Properly."*

*Kaan looked at the turbines standing in their rows against the September sky. For three chapters — foundations, cables, installation — he had watched the wind farm being built from the outside. Steel, copper, concrete, cranes. Now the construction was finished, and the invisible part was about to begin: the electrons, the voltages, the fields. The part you could not see, could not touch, but that made everything else worthwhile.*

*Part IV was over. Part V — the invisible highway — was waiting.*
