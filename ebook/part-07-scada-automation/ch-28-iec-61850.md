# Chapter 28: IEC 61850: The Data Model That Changed Substations

*Kaan's first clue that the SCADA engineering workstation was different from the control room was the printout above the monitor.*

*The control room had printouts too — relay curves, grading tables, compliance matrices, the occasional topology diagram. They were practical documents, used and annotated, updated when something changed. But the printout above the SCADA engineering workstation was different in scale. It was a tree diagram, printed across four A3 sheets taped edge to edge, showing every device in the offshore substation and every wind turbine tower as a branching hierarchy of names: boxes within boxes, descending from a root labelled OSS_SCADA through seventeen levels of indentation to individual data attributes labelled things like XCBR1.Pos.stVal and WTUR1.AlmGrp.stVal.*

*The printout was laminated. Whoever had made it was going to be here for a while.*

*"You're the control engineer," said the woman at the workstation, without turning around. She was Finnish — Kaan had met enough Finnish engineers over the years to recognise the cadence. Precise vowels, sentence-final intonation that was neither interrogative nor assertive, just informational. "Anders said you would ask about the firmware update."*

*Kaan leaned against the doorframe. The room was smaller than the relay testing room, warmer, with the particular smell of electronics under constant power — not hot, just persistent. Two monitors. The left one showed a tree diagram like the one on the wall, navigable, with nodes expanded and collapsed at different depths. The right one showed an XML file: green text on a dark background, repetitive structure, nested angle brackets.*

*"Hanna Virtanen," she said, and turned. Early forties, reading glasses on top of her head, the expression of someone who had explained IEC 61850 to a large number of people and had refined the explanation into something fairly efficient. "I have been configuring IEDs on this standard for fifteen years. The first project was in 2009, at a Finnish utility. The substation used the standard badly. By the fifth project I understood why it is the way it is." She gestured at the chair beside her. "Before you ask about the firmware update, answer one question. What do you think IEC 61850 is?"*

*Kaan thought about what Lars had said the previous morning: a data model that every device in this building understands. "A communication protocol?" he offered.*

*Hanna shook her head. Not with frustration — with the equanimity of someone who had heard this answer many times and had prepared a correction for it. "No. That is the most common misunderstanding in substation automation. IEC 61850 is a data model. The communication is the transport layer. What the standard actually defines — what makes it useful — is not how devices talk to each other. It is what they say." She turned back to her monitor. "The difference matters more than you think."*

## 28.1 Before the Standard: Copper Wires and the Tower of Babel

An offshore substation at 66 kV and 220 kV contains between forty and eighty intelligent electronic devices. The exact count depends on the complexity of the busbar arrangement, the number of transformer and feeder bays, the depth of protection and metering redundancy. A modern OSS serving a 500 MW wind farm — two 250 MVA transformers, eight 66 kV array feeders, one 220 kV export cable connection — might have forty-five to sixty-five IEDs: protection relays, bay controllers, energy meters, protection supervision units, voltage regulators, STATCOM controllers.

Before the mid-1990s, none of those devices could speak to each other without custom integration work.

Every manufacturer had its own communication protocol. Siemens used SINAUT. ABB used SPA-bus. General Electric had its own serial encoding. For European utilities that needed a connection to their energy management systems, IEC 60870-5-101 (serial, RS-232) or DNP3 was common. In North America, DNP3 was the default. In industrial plants, Modbus was often layered on top of everything else. Each protocol defined how data bytes were encoded and sent. None of them defined what the data meant.

The consequence was that a substation required a separate communication processor — a gateway — for each manufacturer's protocol family. A substation with devices from five manufacturers needed five gateways, each mapping that manufacturer's binary output into a representation the SCADA system could interpret. The mapping was built by hand, signal by signal. If a protection relay was replaced after twelve years — say, an old electromechanical relay replaced by a new numerical one — the gateway mapping had to be rebuilt from scratch, even if the new relay did exactly the same job.

There was a second problem that no gateway could solve. Time-critical operations between adjacent IEDs — a protection relay sending a trip command to a bay controller, which must open a circuit breaker within 20 milliseconds — could not travel reliably over serial protocols at 9,600 baud. The latency was too high. So those signals remained hardwired: a copper cable from the relay's trip output contact to the circuit breaker's trip coil. In a large substation, hundreds of hardwired trip and interlock signals ran through cable trays. Each was a potential single point of failure. Each represented an installation cost, a maintenance burden, and a source of error.

The fragility of this arrangement became visible during the 1996 Western grid cascade events in North America. On July 2 and again on August 10, the Western Systems Coordinating Council experienced cascading failures that blacked out large portions of the western grid. The August 10 event cut power to approximately 7.5 million customers across western US states and parts of British Columbia, Alberta, and Baja California.[^1] Post-event analyses identified, among many factors, that the heterogeneous mix of proprietary SCADA protocols meant that data reconstruction after the event required painstaking integration of records from many systems in dissimilar formats. Engineers from different utilities had different pictures of the same unfolding event — not because the data did not exist, but because there was no common model to compare it against.

The EPRI Utility Communications Architecture (UCA) project, which had been developing interoperability concepts since the late 1980s at the Electric Power Research Institute in California, had been working on exactly this problem. By 1999, the project had produced UCA 2.0, a framework that introduced a concept called GSSE — Generic Substation State Events — a mechanism for broadcasting state-change information across a common Ethernet network. The name captured the idea: one message format that any device, from any manufacturer, could subscribe to and understand.

But UCA 2.0 was a research framework, not a standard. In 1995, the International Electrotechnical Commission Technical Committee 57 (IEC TC57) formed three working groups to build on EPRI's work and create a formal international standard. Working Group 10 took functional architecture and requirements; Working Group 11 took communication within and between the unit and station levels; Working Group 12 took communication between the process and unit levels. The three groups worked for nearly a decade, harmonising the North American UCA approach with the European approach (the pivotal meeting was held in Edinburgh in October 1997). The first parts of the resulting standard — IEC 61850 — were published in 2002. The last parts of the first edition appeared in 2005.

The GSSE concept from UCA 2.0 was carried forward into IEC 61850 and refined into a more powerful mechanism: GOOSE, Generic Object-Oriented Substation Event. The evolution from GSSE to GOOSE reflected the new standard's central insight — that the message should not just carry a state bit, but should carry a structured data object whose meaning was defined by the model. Which leads to the model itself.

"In Finland, in 2009, I worked on my first IEC 61850 project," Hanna said. "The utility had decided to implement the standard, but they had done it the wrong way — they used IEC 61850 as a serial protocol replacement. Same point-to-point wiring logic, just with Ethernet cables instead of copper. Same custom gateway per manufacturer. Same configuration nightmare. They had spent a great deal of money to get exactly what they had before, but with newer connectors." She paused. "The mistake was not understanding what the standard actually is."

## 28.2 The Object Model: A Universal Address for Every Data Point

The critical insight of IEC 61850 — the idea that separates it from every protocol that preceded it — is that the standard defines not how devices communicate but what they communicate: a hierarchical data model that every IEC 61850-compliant device must expose, using the same structure, the same names, and the same data types, regardless of manufacturer.

The model has five levels:

**Server** — the top-level object, representing one physical IED. A protection relay is a Server. A bay controller is a Server. The STATCOM's control unit is a Server. Each Server contains one or more Logical Devices.

**Logical Device (LD)** — a named group of related functions within the Server. A single protection relay might have two logical devices: PROT (protection functions) and MEAS (metering functions). The LD name is assigned by the project engineer or manufacturer.

**Logical Node (LN)** — an individual function or capability within the logical device. Every logical node has a class name that defines what it represents: XCBR is a circuit breaker controller; MMXU is a measurement unit; PTOV is an overvoltage protection function; PDIF is a differential protection function. There are over 150 predefined LN classes in IEC 61850-7-4 Edition 2 — the complete functional vocabulary of power system automation.[^2]

**Data Object (DO)** — a specific piece of data within the logical node. The XCBR logical node has a data object called Pos (position). The MMXU logical node has data objects called PhV (phase voltage), A (current), W (active power), and Var (reactive power). Every LN class has a predefined set of data objects that all compliant implementations must expose.

**Data Attribute (DA)** — the actual value within a data object. The Pos data object within XCBR contains three data attributes: stVal (the current state: open, closed, intermediate, bad-state), q (quality flags: validity, overflow, outdated, test mode), and t (timestamp in UTC with microsecond resolution).

The resulting address for any piece of information in the substation follows a fixed structure:

$$
\texttt{LDName} \texttt{/} \texttt{LNClass}\{n\} \texttt{.DOName.DAName}
$$

where:
- `LDName` = logical device name [project-assigned alphanumeric string, max 64 characters]
- `LNClass` = four-character logical node class identifier [standardised by IEC 61850-7-4]
- `{n}` = logical node instance number [integer ≥ 1, mandatory]
- `DOName` = data object name [standardised within the LN class by IEC 61850-7-4]
- `DAName` = data attribute name [standardised: stVal for states, mag.f for magnitudes, q for quality, t for timestamp]

For the 66 kV circuit breaker in Bay 3 of the offshore substation:
- Logical device: `PROT` (protection functions)
- Logical node: `XCBR1` (first circuit breaker controller instance)
- Data object: `Pos` (position)
- Data attribute: `stVal` (current state)

Complete address: `PROT/XCBR1.Pos.stVal`

This address is the same on a Siemens relay, an ABB relay, a GE relay, and a SEL relay. Every manufacturer that implements IEC 61850 correctly places the circuit breaker position at `PROT/XCBR1.Pos.stVal`. A SCADA system or engineering tool from a completely different manufacturer can read the breaker position without any custom mapping, without any project-specific gateway, and without opening any documentation. The class name is the documentation.

Logical node instance names follow a consistent rule:

$$
\texttt{[prefix][LNClass][inst]}
$$

where:
- `prefix` = optional alphanumeric label (up to 11 characters, project-assigned)
- `LNClass` = mandatory four-character class identifier [per IEC 61850-7-4]
- `inst` = mandatory instance number (integer ≥ 1)

So `XCBR1` is the first circuit breaker controller with no prefix; `1XCBR2` is the second circuit breaker controller in bay 1; `PROT1MMXU3` is the third measurement unit in the PROT1 protection context. The naming rule ensures that two instances of the same function in the same logical device are always distinguishable without ambiguity.

In a substation with forty-nine IEDs, each averaging eight logical nodes, there are approximately 390 uniquely addressable functions, each producing multiple data attributes. The OSS at 500 MW generates, in continuous operation, on the order of eight thousand live data attributes across all its IEDs — breaker states, current measurements, voltage phasors, temperatures, alarm flags, protection settings, energy counters. Every one has a permanent, structured address that does not change when the device is replaced.

"The circuit breaker position is always at the same address," Hanna said. She said it flatly, without emphasis, the way someone says a fact they have repeated many times and which has never lost its importance. "That is the whole standard, in seven words."

## 28.3 Logical Node Classes: The Dictionary of a Substation

IEC 61850-7-4 Edition 2 defines over 150 logical node classes, grouped into thirteen functional categories. Edition 1, published in 2003, had approximately 90; the significant expansion in Edition 2 reflects a decade of operational experience and the inclusion of generation, distributed energy resources, and power quality functions not present in the original substation-focused specification.

The thirteen groups cover: System Logical Nodes, Protection, Protection-related, Supervisory Control, Metering and Measurement, Instrumentation, Control, Interfaces and Archiving, Automatic Control, Power Converters, Sensors and Monitoring, Non-Electric Primary Equipment, and Condition Monitoring. Each group has a letter prefix: P for Protection, M for Metering, X for Switching, G for Generic. The four-character class name carries both the prefix and the function: XCBR is X (switching device) + CBR (circuit breaker); MMXU is M (metering) + MXU (measurement unit); PDIF is P (protection) + DIF (differential).

For the OSS at 66 kV and 220 kV, the most frequently encountered logical node classes are:

| LN Class | Function | Typical location |
|----------|----------|-----------------|
| XCBR | Circuit breaker — position, trip/close commands | 66 kV and 220 kV bay IEDs |
| CSWI | Circuit switch controller — interlocking logic | Bay controllers |
| XSWI | Disconnector switch (non-interruptible) | Disconnector relays |
| MMXU | AC measurement — V, I, P, Q, frequency, pf | Metering IEDs, protection relays |
| PTOV | Overvoltage protection — pickup, time dial | 66 kV and 220 kV protection |
| PDIF | Differential protection — bias, restraint | Transformer differential IEDs |
| PIOC | Instantaneous overcurrent — I pickup, operate time | Array feeder protection |
| RREC | Auto-reclose — shot count, dead time, block | Line protection IEDs |
| PDIS | Distance protection — impedance zones, directionality | 220 kV export cable protection |
| GGIO | Generic process I/O — binary inputs/outputs | Miscellaneous and non-standard signals |
| LPHD | Physical device health — temperature, PSU state | Built into every IED |

When a protection engineer opens an SCD file and sees `PDIF1.DifAClc.mag.f`, she knows without opening any manual that it contains the differential current calculation result from the first transformer differential protection instance. The four-character class code is a semantic label that identifies the function's entire purpose.

> **Standard reference:** IEC 61850-7-4:2010 (Edition 2), "Communication networks and systems for power utility automation — Part 7-4: Basic communication structure — Compatible logical node classes and data classes" — Clause 5 (logical node class specifications by functional group) and the normative annexes (mandatory and optional data objects per class). This part is the standard's core reference catalogue; engineers implementing IEC 61850 will use it to verify that each physical function maps to the correct predefined LN class.

The key architectural property is that logical nodes bind function to representation. A relay from 2008 and a relay from 2024, both implementing PDIF to protect the same transformer, expose their differential protection data through the same LN class, the same data object names, and the same data attribute types. The replacement cycle that once required rebuilding every gateway mapping becomes a configuration update: the same LN class, the same address, the same SCADA queries returning the same data structure.

"In Finland, in 2014, we replaced twelve protection relays over three weeks without touching the SCADA configuration once," Hanna said. "We updated the SCD file with the new ICD files from the manufacturer, re-downloaded the CID to each device, and the SCADA saw the same data model it had always seen. No remapping. No integration project." She paused. "The project manager asked if we had done the replacement correctly, because nothing had gone wrong."

## 28.4 SCL: The Substation on Paper

A correctly engineered IEC 61850 system produces a complete digital description of the substation — every device, every logical node, every data attribute, every communication link — before the first piece of hardware is installed. This description is written in the **System Configuration Language (SCL)**, an XML vocabulary defined in IEC 61850-6.

SCL is not a proprietary format. Any SCL-compliant engineering tool from any vendor can read any SCL file produced by any other vendor's tool. The substation's complete data model lives in a text file that is portable across tools, archivable, version-controlled, and comparable between revisions.

Five SCL file types form the engineering workflow, each playing a distinct role:

$$
\text{SSD} + \bigoplus_{i=1}^{N} \text{ICD}_i \;\longrightarrow\; \text{SCD} \;\longrightarrow\; \bigoplus_{i=1}^{N} \text{CID}_i
$$

where $\oplus$ denotes merging: the System Configuration Tool combines the system-level specification (SSD) with the capability files from each device manufacturer (ICD) to produce the master substation description (SCD), which is then split into per-device configured files (CID) downloaded to each IED.

The five types:

**SSD — System Specification Description.** The system engineer describes what the substation needs: voltage levels, number of bays, required protection functions. No specific device is named. The SSD is written before any manufacturer is selected for tendering — it is the electrical design, not the device procurement.

**ICD — IED Capability Description.** The manufacturer supplies one ICD file for each device model, describing every function the device can perform in its full IEC 61850 capability. This file is static — it accompanies the product, like a specification sheet in XML.

**SCD — Substation Configuration Description.** The System Configuration Tool (SCT) merges the SSD with the project's selected ICD files, assigns each device to its bay, instantiates the required logical nodes, and defines the communication links between IEDs. The SCD is the single source of truth for the configured system. Every IED's configuration is visible in one file.

**CID — Configured IED Description.** A device-specific slice of the SCD, containing only the configuration for one IED. This file is downloaded to the device, which validates it against its own ICD. If the CID requests a function the device cannot perform, the validation fails before commissioning — a configuration error caught at the engineering stage, not at site.

**IID — Instantiated IED Description.** The as-built record, produced after factory acceptance testing. It confirms which functions were actually implemented and tested, as opposed to configured.

The SCL approach has an engineering consequence that matters long after commissioning is complete. Because the SCD is a machine-readable description of the entire system — version-controlled and diff-comparable — every change to the substation configuration is a traceable change in an XML file. Configuration drift, the gradual divergence of a system from its documented state through undocumented manual changes, becomes detectable: the SCD at commissioning and the SCD after ten years of operation can be compared line by line.

"The SCD is the most valuable document the project produces," Hanna said. "Not the protection coordination study. Not the cable schedules. The SCD, because it is the living description of what the system knows about itself. If someone changes a protection setting without updating the SCD, the discrepancy will appear at the next configuration audit." She pulled up a diff tool alongside the XML editor. The right panel showed changes highlighted in red and green — a recent parameter update to the STATCOM's setpoint logical node. The change was seven characters in one line. "Seven characters. Visible, traceable, comparable."

## 28.5 IEC 61400-25: The Wind Turbine Joins the Data Model

When IEC 61850 was finalised in its first edition (2002–2005), it was designed with transmission and distribution substations in mind. Wind turbines were not yet large enough to require IEC 61850's level of sophistication, and the specific data a wind turbine produces — rotor speed, pitch angle, yaw position, nacelle temperature, converter DC link voltage — had no predefined logical node class in the base standard.

IEC 61400-25, first published in December 2006, extended the base standard with wind-turbine-specific logical node classes. These classes follow the same architecture as the base standard — same Server/LD/LN/DO/DA hierarchy, same SCL representation, same communication services — but they define the data objects that describe a wind turbine rather than a circuit breaker or transformer.[^3]

The primary wind turbine logical node classes are:

| LN Class | Component | Representative data objects |
|----------|-----------|-----------------------------|
| WTUR | Wind turbine — system level | OpMd (operating mode), TurSt (turbine status), AlmGrp (alarm group) |
| WROT | Wind rotor | RotSpd (rotor speed), BlPtRot (blade pitch rotation), BldAng (blade angle per blade) |
| WGEN | Generator | WGnSpd (generator speed), WGnVol (terminal voltage), WGnAct (active power output) |
| WCNV | Converter (full-scale) | InvSt (inverter status), DcLkV (DC link voltage), InvCtrl (inverter control parameters) |
| WYAW | Yaw system | YawPos (yaw position), YawErr (yaw error), YawCtrl (yaw control mode) |
| WTOW | Tower | TwrAcX (fore-aft acceleration), TwrAcY (side-side acceleration), TwrPrsF (pressure) |
| WNAC | Nacelle | HumTmp (humidity and temperature), NacDir (nacelle direction) |

The complete IEC 61400-25 path for the PLL bandwidth parameter — the forty-three kilobytes that Lars had sent from his laptop to thirty-four turbines — resolves to a specific data attribute address:

$$
\texttt{WTG1LD/WCNV1.InvCtrl.BwPLL.setMag.f}
$$

where:
- `WTG1LD` = the turbine's logical device name [project-assigned: "Wind Turbine Generator 1, Logical Device"]
- `WCNV1` = first converter logical node [IEC 61400-25 class: wind converter, instance 1]
- `InvCtrl` = inverter control parameters [data object standardised within WCNV]
- `BwPLL` = phase-locked loop bandwidth [data attribute set within InvCtrl]
- `setMag.f` = the settable magnitude value [FLOAT32, in Hz; 4.0 for 4 Hz]

Lars's command was a single MMS Write service call to that address on each of thirty-four turbine IEDs. The SCADA server held the IEC 61400-25 logical node map for all thirty-four turbines — every turbine of this model had the same `WCNV1.InvCtrl.BwPLL` data attribute at the same address — and it sent the same structured write request to each. No custom mapping. No gateway. No vessel dispatched. The forty-three kilobytes was the total MMS payload for thirty-four write requests, one per turbine, sequentially acknowledged.

## 28.6 Worked Example: Mapping the OSS IED Tree

**Setup:** Map the complete IEC 61850 logical node tree for one offshore substation serving a 500 MW wind farm. The OSS contains:
- 8 × 66 kV array feeder bay IEDs (protection + metering)
- 2 × 250 MVA transformer bay IEDs (differential protection)
- 2 × transformer bay controller IEDs
- 1 × STATCOM controller IED
- 1 × 220 kV export cable bay IED
- 34 × wind turbine IEDs (IEC 61400-25)
- 1 × Power Plant Controller (PPC) IED

**Step 1 — Count total IEDs:**

$$
N_{\text{IED}} = 8 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 34 + 1 = 49 \text{ IEDs}
$$

**Step 2 — Count logical nodes per IED type:**

| IED type | Key LN classes | LN count per IED |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| 66 kV feeder protection | XCBR, CSWI, PIOC, PTOV, RREC, MMXU, LPHD | ~8 |
| Transformer differential | PDIF, PTOV, MMXU, LPHD | ~6 |
| Transformer bay controller | XCBR, CSWI, XSWI, MMXU, LPHD | ~8 |
| 220 kV export bay | XCBR, CSWI, PDIS, PTOV, MMXU, LPHD | ~10 |
| STATCOM controller | GGIO (setpoints), MMXU, LPHD | ~8 |
| Wind turbine IED | WTUR, WROT, WGEN, WCNV, WYAW, WTOW, WNAC, LPHD | ~8 |
| PPC IED | GGIO (farm setpoints), MMXU (farm measurements), LPHD | ~10 |

**Step 3 — Total logical nodes:**

$$
N_{\text{LN}} = (8 \times 8) + (2 \times 6) + (2 \times 8) + (1 \times 10) + (1 \times 8) + (34 \times 8) + (1 \times 10)
$$

$$
= 64 + 12 + 16 + 10 + 8 + 272 + 10 = 392 \text{ logical nodes}
$$

**Step 4 — Estimate total data attributes:**

Each logical node contains on average 5–8 data objects, each with 3–6 data attributes (stVal + q + t for status; mag.f + q + t for measurements; setMag.f + minVal + maxVal for settings). Using a conservative average of 20 data attributes per LN:

$$
N_{\text{DA}} = N_{\text{LN}} \times 20 = 392 \times 20 \approx 7{,}840 \text{ live data attributes}
$$

This is the addressable data model of the substation. Every one of the approximately 7,840 data attributes has a permanent, structured IEC 61850 address — readable by any compliant engineering tool, without gateway, without mapping document.

**Step 5 — SCL XML fragment (Bay 3 circuit breaker):**

The SCD file represents the Bay 3 protection relay as a logical node instance. The following SCL fragment shows the XCBR logical node within the relay's protection logical device:

```xml
<IED name="REF615_Bay03" type="REF615" manufacturer="ABB" configVersion="1.0">
  <Services>
    <ReportSettings rptID="true" optFields="true" />
    <GOOSE max="4" />
    <MMS />
  </Services>
  <AccessPoint name="P1">
    <Server>
      <LDevice inst="PROT">
        <LN0 lnClass="LLN0" inst="" lnType="LLN0_type1" />
        <LN lnClass="LPHD" inst="1" lnType="LPHD_type1" />
        <LN lnClass="XCBR" inst="1" lnType="XCBR_type1"
            desc="66kV Circuit Breaker Bay 03" />
        <LN lnClass="CSWI" inst="1" lnType="CSWI_type1" />
        <LN lnClass="MMXU" inst="1" lnType="MMXU_type1" />
        <LN lnClass="PIOC" inst="1" lnType="PIOC_type1" />
      </LDevice>
    </Server>
  </AccessPoint>
</IED>
```

The four-character LN class names (XCBR, CSWI, MMXU, PIOC) are mandated by IEC 61850-7-4. The inst attributes (instance numbers) and desc strings are project-assigned. An engineering tool reading this file knows, without any manual, that XCBR1.Pos.stVal contains the circuit breaker's current state, because the class name is the manual.

**Step 6 — Answer the firmware update question:**

The SCADA server held the IEC 61400-25 logical node tree for all thirty-four turbines. For each turbine, it sent a single MMS Write service request:

- Target: `WTGiLD/WCNV1.InvCtrl.BwPLL.setMag.f` for i = 1 to 34
- Value: 4.0 (FLOAT32, units Hz)
- Service: SetDataValue (ACSI service mapped to MMS PDU)

Each turbine IED validated the write against its own ICD — confirming that BwPLL was a settable parameter, type FLOAT32, range 0.5–15.0 Hz — and applied the new value. Total elapsed time: sixteen minutes, limited by the sequential write acknowledgement protocol over the 66 kV array fibre network. Thirty-four individual controller updates. No panel access, no manual steps, no vessels dispatched.

The data model — not the protocol — made this possible. MMS was the vehicle. IEC 61400-25 was the map.

## Key Takeaways

- **IEC 61850 is a data model, not a protocol.** It defines what substation data means — the five-level object hierarchy (Server → LD → LN → DO → DA) and the logical node class catalogue — independently of how that data is transmitted. Any communication transport that implements the model correctly can carry it.

- **Logical node classes are a manufacturer-independent vocabulary.** `XCBR1.Pos.stVal` is the circuit breaker position on any IEC 61850-compliant device, from any manufacturer, in any country. Replacing a protection relay is a configuration update, not a system integration project.

- **SCL files give the substation a machine-readable description.** The SSD, ICD, SCD, and CID files encode the complete design and configuration of the system in an XML vocabulary that any compliant engineering tool can read, diff, and version-control. Configuration drift becomes detectable.

- **IEC 61400-25 extends the data model to wind turbines.** Wind-specific logical node classes (WTUR, WROT, WGEN, WCNV, WYAW, WTOW, WNAC) give every component of a wind turbine a standard address in the IEC 61850 object tree. A SCADA server that knows the turbine model's ICD can address any parameter — including the PLL bandwidth sent from Lars's laptop — without custom integration work.

- **The standard converts the substation's information into an engineering discipline.** The approximately 7,840 data attributes in a 500 MW OSS do not only describe the physical state of the substation — they describe it consistently, completely, and in a format that survives equipment replacement, staff turnover, and software upgrades. The data model outlives the hardware.

*The SCD file that Hanna had open on her right monitor was 22,847 lines long. She navigated it without using search — pressing the arrow keys through collapsed and expanded XML blocks the way someone else might turn pages of a familiar book.*

*"It is strange," Kaan said, looking at the tree diagram on the wall — all four A3 sheets of it, thousands of labelled boxes connected by lines. "From the outside, the substation looks like a building full of equipment. From here it looks like a data structure."*

*"It is both," Hanna said. "The equipment converts electricity. The data model converts the equipment's behaviour into something that can be reasoned about. You cannot send a protection trip over Ethernet without the first. You cannot update thirty-four PLL bandwidths from a laptop without the second." She closed the SCD and opened a new file — labelled WTG_Turbine_Generic_R2.icd, a manufacturer's ICD for the V236 model. "The turbines have their own ICD files. Everything they can report is in the tree on the wall." She pointed at the bottom-left corner of the four-sheet printout, where thirty-four small clusters of boxes were labelled WTG1 through WTG34. "Thirty-four identical subsets of the same model."*

*Kaan studied the tree. The logical nodes were recognisable now: XCBR, MMXU, PDIF in the substation blocks, and in each turbine cluster, WTUR, WROT, WGEN, WCNV. The tree was not documentation of the farm — it was the farm, in the only form that a computer could reason about.*

*"This is how the farm speaks to itself," he said.*

*"That is correct," Hanna said. "Tomorrow: how it shouts."*

*GOOSE — the Generic Object-Oriented Substation Event, evolved from the GSSE mechanism that EPRI engineers had first proposed in the late 1990s, refined by three IEC working groups over seven years into something faster and more expressive — was the word she did not say. The trip signal that could not be trusted to a serial protocol. The message that a protection relay would multicast to every subscriber on the Ethernet segment in under four milliseconds — not because the recipient asked for it, not because a master polled for it, but because the event had happened and the farm's nervous system needed to know. The shouting, when it came, was the subject of the next day's session.*

*Kaan walked back to the control room. The OSS hum was unchanged: transformers, HVAC, the STATCOM's coolant fans. But behind it, invisible and continuous, forty-nine IEDs were exchanging data attributes at a rate of several thousand per second. The substation had always been speaking in this language. He was only now learning to read it.*
