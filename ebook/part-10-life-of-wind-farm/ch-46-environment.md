## 46.1 The Reef That No One Planned

The aggregation of marine life around offshore structures is not a new observation. Japanese coastal fishing communities deliberately sank bamboo bundles and clay jars to attract shellfish and demersal fish as early as the sixteenth century; the first systematic scientific descriptions of the phenomenon date from observations of oil platforms in the Gulf of Mexico in the 1940s and 1950s. By the 1970s, the United States National Fisheries Service was conducting surveys of the fish assemblages around drilling platforms in the Gulf of Mexico and documenting fish densities that, in some instances, exceeded the surrounding natural seabed by two orders of magnitude.

The mechanism has two proposed explanations that are not mutually exclusive. The **aggregation hypothesis** holds that structures attract fish from the surrounding environment — that the fish counted around a structure represent the same fish that would otherwise be distributed across the adjacent seabed. The **production hypothesis** holds that the vertical structure creates new habitat that is genuinely not present on flat sandy seabeds — that the colonisation by filter-feeding invertebrates (mussels, barnacles, tunicates) produces organic material and shelter that supports animals that could not have existed there before. At most offshore installations studied to date, the evidence suggests both effects operate simultaneously, with the production contribution more significant over longer time periods as the fouling community matures and establishes trophic relationships that the flat sandy seabed cannot support.

The colonisation sequence follows a predictable ecology:

**Stage 1 (Weeks 1–8): Biofilm and diatoms.** Bacteria and diatoms coat the steel surface within weeks of immersion. The biofilm produces chemical signals that attract invertebrate larvae. No structure at this stage would be visible to a casual observer.

**Stage 2 (Months 2–12): Pioneer invertebrates.** Barnacles, mussels, and tube worms settle on the biofilm layer. In the Baltic, with its lower salinity relative to the North Sea, the dominant pioneer species are blue mussel (*Mytilus edulis*) and acorn barnacle (*Semibalanus balanoides*). The surface area available on a 10-metre diameter monopile from mudline to surface is approximately 1,900 m² — equivalent to a half-hectare of vertical reef habitat per turbine.[^1]

**Stage 3 (Years 1–5): Secondary colonisers and fish.** Kelp (*Laminaria saccharina* and *Saccharina latissima*) establishes in well-lit zones above the halocline. The mussel bed provides prey for foraging fish and provides physical structure for demersal species. Eelpout, sand eels, and juvenile cod appear at stages two to three; larger cod, ballan wrasse, and saithe in the third year and beyond.

**Stage 4 (Years 5–30): Mature reef community.** A diverse multi-layered community develops, with predator-prey relationships between assemblages at different depth zones. At Horns Rev I (commissioned 2002, first systematic biological surveys 2005–2010), researchers documented eighty species of invertebrates and thirty-six species of fish in and around the wind farm, compared to an average of twelve fish species in adjacent reference areas.[^2]

The Horns Rev I finding is the benchmark study. In 2009, surveys showed fish biomass densities around the monopile bases that were four to eighty times higher than in adjacent unfished sandy reference areas, depending on the species and measurement method. The most striking finding was not the filter feeders — expected for any hard substrate — but the cod: *Gadus morhua* aggregating in numbers that marine biologists working in the North Sea had not observed in comparable habitat outside protected marine reserves.

A fun fact worth stating plainly: in some areas of the North Sea, the inside of an offshore wind farm has become one of the most productive fish habitats in the region — not because of any deliberate management decision but because the exclusion of trawling vessels (for operational safety reasons) has created, accidentally, a set of de facto marine protected areas across several hundred square kilometres of previously heavily trawled seabed.

## 46.2 What Was Measured Before: Environmental Assessment

No offshore wind farm in the European Union can begin construction without a completed Environmental Impact Assessment, and no EIA can begin without a baseline survey of what exists before the installation starts. The regulatory requirement is embedded in the EU EIA Directive (Directive 2011/92/EU, as amended by 2014/52/EU), which mandates assessment of significant effects on biodiversity, the marine environment, human health, cultural heritage, and the interactions between them.[^3]

For a facility of this scale — over 100 MW, located in marine waters, with foundations requiring seabed alteration — the EIA is a multi-year process. The survey programme for a typical large offshore wind project in the Baltic region includes:

**Benthic surveys:** Grab samples and trawl surveys of the seabed community at the planned turbine and cable positions. For this farm, the pre-construction benthic surveys revealed an impoverished sandy seabed community typical of the central Baltic — primarily bivalves, polychaete worms, and amphipods — with low species richness compared to comparable sites in the North Sea. The reef effect observed at Month 18 therefore represents a genuine habitat creation, not a displacement of existing hard-substrate communities.

**Acoustic surveys for harbour porpoises:** T-POD and C-POD click detectors deployed at multiple sites across the farm area for a minimum of twelve months, recording echolocation clicks from *Phocoena phocoena* to establish baseline presence, frequency, and temporal patterns. The harbour porpoise is the only cetacean species for which the baseline acoustic survey is legally mandated under the EU Habitats Directive (92/43/EEC) in Baltic waters, because the Baltic Proper subspecies (*P. p. relicta*) is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN with a population estimated at fewer than five hundred individuals.[^4]

**Avian surveys:** Vessel-based transect surveys and shore-based radar monitoring to characterise the species composition, flight height distribution, and seasonal variation in bird abundance across and around the proposed farm area. For this farm, the site is adjacent to a migration corridor used by sea ducks (primarily long-tailed duck, *Clangula hyemalis*, and common eider, *Somateria mollissima*) during their autumn passage from Baltic breeding grounds to the North Sea and Atlantic overwintering areas. Seasonal timing restrictions during the peak migration period (October–November) were embedded in the construction programme as a condition of the EIA consent.

**Natura 2000 proximity assessment:** The EU Habitats Directive requires a Habitats Regulations Assessment (HRA) — also known as an Appropriate Assessment — for any plan or project that may have a significant effect on a Natura 2000 site, either alone or in combination with other projects. The nearest Natura 2000 site to this farm is designated for harbour porpoise and sea eagle (*Haliaeetus albicilla*); the farm's boundary was drawn specifically to maintain a 5 km exclusion zone from the nearest designated habitat.

> **Standard reference:** Directive 2011/92/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 December 2011 on the assessment of the effects of certain public and private projects on the environment (codification). Article 3 defines the scope of assessment; Article 4 and Annex II specify the criteria for screening. Amended by Directive 2014/52/EU.

## 46.3 Construction Impacts and Mitigation

Of all the physical impacts associated with offshore wind construction, one dominates the permitting and mitigation planning: **impulsive underwater noise from pile driving**. No other construction activity produces comparable sound pressure levels across the low-frequency range where marine mammals' hearing is most sensitive.

The physical source is a hydraulic impact hammer striking the top of the steel monopile. Each blow propagates energy through the pile wall as a compression wave, radiates into the surrounding water as an acoustic impulse, and propagates outward in all directions. In shallow water — typical of Baltic sites at 30–60 m depth — the propagation is transitional between spherical spreading (energy decreasing as 1/r²) close to the source and cylindrical spreading (energy decreasing as 1/r) at greater ranges, because the sea surface and seabed confine the sound field vertically.

The standard model for transmission loss in this regime uses a frequency-dependent spreading coefficient:

$$
TL(r) = k \cdot \log_{10}\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right) + \alpha \cdot (r - r_0)
$$

where:
- $TL(r)$ = transmission loss at range $r$ from the source [dB]
- $r_0$ = reference range (typically 1 m) [m]
- $k$ = geometric spreading exponent [dimensionless] — 20 for spherical spreading in deep water, 10 for cylindrical spreading, 15–18 for the shallow-water transitional regime
- $\alpha$ = frequency-dependent absorption coefficient [dB/m] — approximately 0.002–0.005 dB/m in the 100–1,000 Hz range relevant to pile driving

The received sound level at range $r$ from a source with source level $SL$ is then:

$$
RL(r) = SL - TL(r)
$$

where:
- $RL(r)$ = received sound pressure level at range $r$ [dB re 1 µPa]
- $SL$ = source level at 1 m from the pile [dB re 1 µPa]

Two metrics are used simultaneously in all European regulatory frameworks:

- **SPL_peak**: the maximum instantaneous sound pressure level, in decibels relative to 1 µPa. Peaks are short, intense, and potentially startle-inducing.
- **SEL (Sound Exposure Level)**: the sound energy integrated over the duration of a single impulse, in decibels relative to 1 µPa²·s. SEL captures the energy dose, which is the physiologically relevant metric for hearing damage.

The German Federal Maritime and Hydrographic Agency (BSH) published the first binding impulsive noise standard for offshore wind installation in 2011, since updated, and it has been widely adopted across the Baltic region through bilateral agreements and national transposition:[^5]

- SEL ≤ 160 dB re 1 µPa²·s at 750 m from the source, for a single hammer strike
- SPL_peak ≤ 190 dB re 1 µPa at 750 m from the source

Both criteria must be satisfied simultaneously.

For assessments of cumulative exposure — when an animal might remain within range for multiple strikes — the cumulative sound exposure level adds up the energy from $N$ individual strikes:

$$
SEL_{cum} = SEL_{single} + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(N)
$$

where:
- $SEL_{cum}$ = cumulative sound exposure level over $N$ strikes [dB re 1 µPa²·s]
- $SEL_{single}$ = single-strike sound exposure level at the assessment point [dB re 1 µPa²·s]
- $N$ = number of hammer strikes [dimensionless]

The principal mitigation tool is the **bubble curtain**: a ring of perforated hose surrounding the pile at the seabed, through which compressed air is pumped to create a curtain of rising bubbles around the entire circumference. The impedance mismatch between air bubbles and water scatters and absorbs acoustic energy in the relevant frequency range, attenuating the outward-propagating impulse. A well-engineered double bubble curtain — two concentric hose rings, with the outer ring at the boundary of the scour protection area — achieves an insertion loss of 15–22 dB in Baltic water conditions, depending on the bubble curtain design, water depth, and the frequency content of the hammer.[^6]

Additional mitigation layers are operational, not acoustic:

**Soft start protocol:** The hammer begins at 10% of its maximum strike energy and increases to full energy over the first 30 minutes, giving animals that cannot hear or respond to the bubble curtain alone sufficient time to move outside the exclusion zone.

**Marine mammal observer (MMO):** A trained observer on the installation vessel conducts a 30-minute visual observation period before piling begins. If a harbour porpoise or other cetacean is observed within 1,500 m of the pile position, piling is halted and does not resume until the animal has moved outside the exclusion zone and the MMO has confirmed its absence for a further 30 minutes.

**Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM):** Hydrophone arrays are deployed continuously for the duration of the installation campaign, providing automatic cetacean detection regardless of visibility or observation conditions.

**Seasonal timing restrictions:** No pile driving was conducted between 1 October and 30 November to avoid the peak sea duck migration period. This constraint was embedded in the construction programme as a consent condition, not as an operational choice.

## 46.4 The Operational Environment

Once installed and generating, the environmental footprint of an offshore wind farm changes character completely. The dominant construction impacts — impulsive noise, vessel traffic, seabed disturbance — are replaced by a very different set of low-level, continuous interactions.

**Underwater radiated noise in operation:** At typical operating conditions, the noise radiated through the monopile foundations into the water is broadband and continuous but spectrally unremarkable. The dominant sources are drivetrain vibration (at frequencies related to the rotor, gearbox, and generator) transmitted through the tower and foundation into the surrounding water. Measured levels at 100 m range are typically 120–140 dB re 1 µPa RMS — below the ambient noise level in moderate sea states and well below the threshold for behavioural disturbance in harbour porpoises.[^7]

**Electromagnetic fields from array cables:** The 66 kV array cables running between turbines carry three-phase alternating current. A single energised cable produces a magnetic field that falls off inversely with distance from the conductor. For a balanced three-phase cable buried in trefoil configuration (the three conductors arranged in a triangle with centroid at the cable centre), the fields from the three conductors are approximately equal in magnitude and displaced by 120° in phase. At distances large compared to the cable's individual conductor spacing, the three fields add to near zero:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 \cdot I}{2\pi r}
$$

where:
- $B$ = magnetic flux density from a single isolated conductor [T]
- $\mu_0$ = permeability of free space = $4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m/A
- $I$ = cable current [A]
- $r$ = perpendicular distance from conductor [m]

For a trefoil-laid cable, the three conductors are separated by approximately 90–120 mm. At 1 m above the cable burial depth, the three fields cancel to a residual of approximately 1–5 µT — well below the 40 µT threshold established by ICNIRP (International Commission on Non-Ionising Radiation Protection) for continuous human exposure, and in a range that laboratory studies have not demonstrated to cause navigational disruption in elasmobranch species (sharks and rays) that use Earth's magnetic field for orientation, though the question remains actively studied.[^8]

**Bird collision risk:** Collision mortality is the operational interaction most difficult to eliminate by design. Once turbines are rotating, birds that fly through the rotor swept area at rotor height during hours of poor visibility or high flight intensity risk collision with a blade. The risk is estimated using the Band collision risk model (Band 2000, refined by Smales 2019), which calculates the expected number of collision fatalities per species per year as a function of bird density, flight speed, rotor geometry, and avoidance rate:

$$
N_{col} = \frac{\Phi \cdot 2R \cdot p_h \cdot T}{v_f} \cdot P_{col} \cdot (1 - F_{avoidance})
$$

where:
- $N_{col}$ = expected collisions per species per year [yr$^{-1}$]
- $\Phi$ = bird flux density — individuals per metre of rotor diameter per hour at risk altitude [m$^{-1}$·h$^{-1}$]
- $R$ = rotor radius [m]
- $p_h$ = proportion of flight hours occurring at risk altitude (hub height ± rotor radius) [dimensionless]
- $T$ = turbine operating hours per year [h·yr$^{-1}$]
- $v_f$ = mean bird flight speed [m·s$^{-1}$]
- $P_{col}$ = probability of collision given transit of the rotor disk, a function of the rotor geometry and bird wingspan [dimensionless]
- $F_{avoidance}$ = large-scale avoidance rate — the proportion of birds that deviate before entering the rotor disk [dimensionless]

The avoidance rate term dominates the result. For most species, $F_{avoidance}$ lies between 0.95 and 0.999 — that is, between one in twenty and one in a thousand birds that approach a turbine actually strike it. The Band model result is therefore extremely sensitive to the avoidance rate assumption, which makes post-construction mortality monitoring (turbine base searches and scavenger correction factors) essential for validating the pre-construction impact assessment. For this farm, the primary species of concern is the white-tailed sea eagle (*Haliaeetus albicilla*) — a breeding species in coastal Poland with an avoidance rate estimated from radar tracking data at approximately 0.988, yielding an expected mortality of less than one bird per farm per year.[^9]

## 46.5 End of Life: The Decommissioning Question

The environmental impact assessment for an offshore wind farm does not end at commissioning. From the first day of construction consent, the developer holds a legal obligation to restore the site at the end of the project's life — to remove the structures, to clear the seabed, and to confirm, through monitoring, that the marine environment has returned to a condition comparable to its pre-installation baseline.

This obligation exists because the seabed of the Exclusive Economic Zone is not the developer's property. It is leased from the crown or state for a defined period — typically twenty-five to thirty years, corresponding to the turbines' design life and the duration of the financial support mechanism. At the end of that period, the lease expires and the obligation to restore the site becomes due.

The practical challenge is formidable. A 15 MW turbine rests on a foundation that extends twenty to forty metres into the seabed, with a steel monopile twelve to fifteen metres in diameter and five to six centimetres of wall thickness. The installed mass of steel per turbine — foundation, tower, nacelle, and blades — is approximately 2,800 to 3,500 tonnes for a machine of this class. Across thirty-four turbines, the total steel mass on site is approximately 100,000 to 120,000 tonnes: equivalent to twenty Eiffel Towers, permanently embedded in the seabed of the Polish Baltic.

Three options exist for the disposition of each structure at end of life:

**Option 1: Full removal.** The monopile is cut below the mudline using an abrasive water jet or mechanical cutting tool, lifted by a jack-up vessel, and transported to a recycling facility. The scour protection rock is collected and removed or dispersed. The seabed is confirmed clear by side-scan sonar survey. This is the legally required outcome under OSPAR Decision 98/3 for installations in the OSPAR maritime area (North-East Atlantic) and under analogous HELCOM Recommendation 10/3E for Baltic installations — neither permits abandonment except under specifically defined exemptions.[^10]

**Option 2: Partial removal.** The turbine, tower, and all above-seabed structure is removed; the monopile foundation is cut at 1–3 m below the mudline, leaving the pile skirt in place. This option is sometimes permitted by national regulators as a pragmatic compromise: the seabed surface is clear (no navigational hazard), but the embedded steel remains in place indefinitely. Whether the 2,400 tonnes of embedded steel per turbine is considered "removal" varies by national interpretation of the OSPAR/HELCOM framework.

**Option 3: Leave in place — the reef exemption.** A developer who can demonstrate that the ecological value of the foundation community — the reef — exceeds the ecological cost of removal may apply for an exemption from the removal obligation. Several North Sea countries are actively developing the policy and evidential framework for such applications: the Netherlands' "Windpark als Natuur" (Wind Farm as Nature) initiative has established a pilot process for exactly this purpose, with monitoring obligations and adaptive management conditions attached to any exemption granted.

The reef exemption debate is active precisely because the evidence is now compelling. At Horns Rev I, the Danish Energy Agency commissioned a cost-benefit analysis of full removal versus reef exemption in 2023. The ecological value of the reef communities — estimated through a natural capital accounting framework that assigned monetary values to biomass production, biodiversity support, and ecosystem services — exceeded the avoided cost of removal (the decommissioning cost that would not be incurred) by a factor of three to five across a range of assumptions.[^11] At the same time, the regulatory framework inherited from oil and gas decommissioning was designed to prevent negligent abandonment of structures that posed genuine environmental and navigational hazard — not to prevent the preservation of an ecologically beneficial artificial reef.

The decommissioning cost itself is estimated as a fraction of the original installation cost, inflated to the decommissioning year:

$$
C_{decom} = f_{decom} \cdot C_{install} \cdot e^{r_{esc} \cdot n}
$$

where:
- $C_{decom}$ = decommissioning cost at year $n$ of operation [EUR]
- $f_{decom}$ = decommissioning cost factor [dimensionless] — typically 0.4 to 0.9 for offshore wind, depending on the technology generation, water depth, and removal method. Full monopile removal from 40–50 m water is estimated at 0.7–0.9 of installation cost; partial removal or reef exemption reduces this to 0.2–0.4.
- $C_{install}$ = original installation cost [EUR] — foundations, turbines, cables, and installation
- $r_{esc}$ = cost escalation rate [yr$^{-1}$] — industry estimates typically 2–3% per year for specialised marine construction
- $n$ = years from commissioning to decommissioning [yr]

The financial obligation embedded in the decommissioning clause is substantial and long-dated. From the moment the farm is commissioned, the developer begins accumulating a liability that matures twenty-five to thirty years in the future. If that liability is not provisioned, the developer at end of life may lack the resources to fulfil the obligation — leaving the cost to the state or, effectively, stranding the structures permanently.

Standard project finance arrangements require the establishment of a **decommissioning trust fund** or equivalent provision: a segregated account into which the developer makes annual contributions, sufficient to accumulate — with investment returns — the full estimated decommissioning cost by the decommissioning date. The annual provision is the present-value annuity payment required to fund the liability:

$$
A_{decom} = C_{decom,PV} \cdot \frac{WACC}{1 - (1 + WACC)^{-n}}
$$

where:
- $A_{decom}$ = annual decommissioning provision [EUR·yr$^{-1}$]
- $C_{decom,PV}$ = present value of decommissioning cost at WACC [EUR]
- $WACC$ = weighted average cost of capital [yr$^{-1}$]
- $n$ = remaining years to decommissioning [yr]

## 46.6 Worked Example — Pile Driving Noise: From Source to Compliance

**Scenario:** Installation of a 10-metre diameter monopile for a 15 MW class turbine in 42 m water depth. Hydraulic impact hammer with maximum strike energy 3,000 kJ, operating at 38 strikes per minute at full energy.

**Source levels** (empirical, based on published measurements for large monopiles in comparable water depths):

| Metric | Source Level at 1 m |
|--------|---------------------|
| SPL_peak | 248 dB re 1 µPa |
| SEL (single strike) | 232 dB re 1 µPa²·s |

---

**Step 1: Transmission loss at 750 m**

For 42 m water depth with a sandy seabed and salinity of 10 PSU, the shallow-water spreading exponent $k$ = 17 is appropriate (intermediate regime). Absorption coefficient $\alpha$ = 0.003 dB/m at the dominant pile driving frequencies of 100–500 Hz.

$$TL(750) = 17 \cdot \log_{10}(750) + 0.003 \cdot 750 = 17 \times 2.875 + 2.25 = 51.1\ \text{dB}$$

---

**Step 2: Unmitigated received levels at 750 m**

$$SPL_{peak,rec} = 248 - 51.1 = \mathbf{196.9}\ \text{dB re 1}\ \mu\text{Pa} \quad [+6.9\ \text{dB above BSH 190 dB limit}]$$
$$SEL_{single,rec} = 232 - 51.1 = \mathbf{180.9}\ \text{dB re 1}\ \mu\text{Pa}^2\text{·s} \quad [+20.9\ \text{dB above BSH 160 dB limit}]$$

Without mitigation, the SEL criterion is exceeded by 20.9 dB. The installation cannot proceed in this configuration.

---

**Step 3: Required bubble curtain insertion loss**

The SEL criterion governs (20.9 dB excess vs 6.9 dB for SPL_peak). The bubble curtain must achieve at least 21 dB insertion loss to bring the SEL below 160 dB. A double large-bubble curtain system — two concentric perforated hose rings at the outer edge of the scour protection, driven by five air compressors — was specified for this installation based on published performance data and acoustic modelling. Design target: 22 dB insertion loss.

---

**Step 4: Mitigated received levels at 750 m**

$$SPL_{peak,rec,mit} = 196.9 - 22 = \mathbf{174.9}\ \text{dB re 1}\ \mu\text{Pa} \quad [\checkmark\ \text{vs 190 dB limit}]$$
$$SEL_{single,rec,mit} = 180.9 - 22 = \mathbf{158.9}\ \text{dB re 1}\ \mu\text{Pa}^2\text{·s} \quad [\checkmark\ \text{vs 160 dB limit}]$$

Both criteria satisfied with 15.1 dB margin on SPL_peak and 1.1 dB margin on SEL. The SEL margin of 1.1 dB is deliberately tight — the bubble curtain system was sized to meet the standard, not to over-engineer it, because every additional compressor and hose segment increases the daily setup time and the operational risk of air supply failure.

---

**Step 5: Cumulative SEL assessment at the 750 m monitoring position**

For a harbour porpoise that does not move during a continuous 30-minute piling session at full energy (conservative assumption, used for permitting — the soft start protocol and MMO/PAM system reduce this risk in practice):

$$N = 38\ \text{strikes/min} \times 30\ \text{min} = 1{,}140\ \text{strikes}$$
$$SEL_{cum} = 158.9 + 10 \cdot \log_{10}(1{,}140) = 158.9 + 30.6 = \mathbf{189.5}\ \text{dB re 1}\ \mu\text{Pa}^2\text{·s}$$

The NOAA permanent threshold shift (PTS) onset criterion for small toothed whales (phocoenids: $SEL_{cum} \geq 185$ dB re 1 µPa²·s) is exceeded at 750 m even with the bubble curtain, if an animal remains stationary for the full 30 minutes. This is why the bubble curtain is not the only mitigation measure. The 30-minute pre-piling observation period, the MMO protocol, and the PAM hydrophone array collectively ensure that no animal remains at close range before and during active pile driving. The standard is designed to be met by the combination of all measures together — not by the bubble curtain in isolation.

---

**Step 6: Decommissioning cost estimate**

Installation cost (foundations + cables + turbines + installation vessels, from Chapter 15): approximately EUR 900M for the marine construction components.

Decommissioning cost factor for full monopile removal in 42 m water: $f_{decom}$ = 0.75 (mid-range estimate for this generation of equipment and water depth).

Cost escalation at 2.5% per year over 28 years (from commissioning to planned decommissioning):

$$C_{decom} = 0.75 \times 900 \times e^{0.025 \times 28} = 675 \times e^{0.70} = 675 \times 2.014 = \text{EUR}\ 1{,}359\text{M}$$

Present value at WACC 5.73%, 28 years:

$$C_{decom,PV} = \frac{1{,}359}{(1.0573)^{28}} = \frac{1{,}359}{4.79} = \text{EUR}\ 284\text{M}$$

Annual decommissioning provision:

$$A_{decom} = 284 \times \frac{0.0573}{1-(1.0573)^{-28}} = 284 \times \frac{0.0573}{0.791} = \text{EUR}\ 20.6\text{M/yr}$$

At EUR 20.6 million per year — approximately 10% of the farm's annual gross revenue — the decommissioning provision is a material cost embedded from the first year of operation, not a distant liability to be worried about later. The trust fund is separate from operating cash flows and is not available to the project company for any other purpose.

|-----------|-------|
| Full-removal decommissioning cost (Year 28) | EUR 1,359M |

| Annual provision (annuity, Years 1–28) | EUR 20.6M/yr |
| As % of annual gross revenue | ~10% |

If the reef exemption were granted and only partial removal required ($f_{decom}$ = 0.3), the decommissioning cost would fall to approximately EUR 544M in Year 28, reducing the annual provision to EUR 8.3M/yr. The financial case for the reef exemption is therefore substantial — and it aligns with the ecological evidence. The policy question is whether that case has been demonstrated convincingly enough for the regulatory framework to accommodate it.

## Key Takeaways

- **The reef effect is an unintended consequence of solving the foundation engineering correctly.** The 1,900 m² of vertical hard substrate per monopile creates habitat that does not naturally exist on the flat sandy Baltic seabed. The colonisation sequence — biofilm to mussels to kelp to fish — is ecologically rapid: meaningful hard-substrate communities can be documented within twelve to eighteen months of installation, and mature reef communities, supporting significantly elevated fish biomass relative to reference areas, within five years. The aggregation of depleted Baltic cod at the scour protection rock is not a curiosity — it is evidence that the reef effect is genuinely productive, not merely redistributive.

- **Pile driving is the dominant environmental impact of offshore wind construction, not operation.** The BSH impulsive noise standard (SEL ≤ 160 dB re 1 µPa²·s at 750 m) cannot be met without active mitigation for monopiles of current commercial dimensions. A double bubble curtain, soft start protocol, marine mammal observer, and passive acoustic monitoring system operate together as a mitigation package; no single measure is sufficient. This engineering-plus-monitoring approach is the standard across the North Sea and Baltic.

- **The Habitats Directive and EIA Directive impose obligations that begin before the first vessel is mobilised and extend beyond the last turbine is decommissioned.** Pre-construction surveys establish the baseline; post-construction monitoring tracks departure from it. Natura 2000 proximity determines whether an Appropriate Assessment is required. The harbour porpoise — specifically, the Baltic Proper subspecies at fewer than five hundred individuals — is the most legally significant protected species in Baltic offshore wind permitting.

- **Decommissioning is not an end-of-life problem — it is a Year 1 financial obligation.** The present value of the decommissioning liability, discounted at WACC, must be provisioned annually from commissioning. At approximately EUR 20 million per year for a 500 MW farm with full-removal obligations, the trust fund contribution represents approximately 10% of gross annual revenue — a material cost that must be built into the original project finance model. Projects that do not provision adequately are leaving an unfunded liability to the state or to future project owners.

- **The reef exemption debate will determine whether offshore wind's environmental legacy is removal or reef.** As the first generation of North Sea wind farms approaches end of life (Vindeby was decommissioned in 2017; Middelgrunden and Horns Rev I are approaching the end of their original design lives), the evidence base for the production hypothesis has strengthened to the point where natural capital valuations consistently exceed removal costs. Whether the regulatory framework — designed for oil and gas abandonment prevention — can be adapted quickly enough to accommodate this evidence is the policy question that will define the ecological legacy of the first generation of offshore wind energy.

## For Further Reading

- **Stenberg, C., van Deurs, M., Støttrup, J. G., Mosegaard, H., Grome, T. M., Dinesen, G., & Leonhard, S. B. (2015).** "Effect of the Horns Rev 1 Offshore Wind Farm on Fish Communities — Follow-up Seven Years After Construction." *DTU Aqua Report No. 290-2015.* Technical University of Denmark, Lyngby. Available at orbit.dtu.dk. The most comprehensive fish ecology study at any European offshore wind farm, tracking species composition, biomass, and distribution inside Horns Rev I and at six reference stations from 2005 to 2012 — seven years post-installation. The report documents the emergence of a structurally distinct fish community inside the wind farm, characterised by higher biomass, greater species diversity, and significant cod aggregation relative to reference areas. The multi-year time series covers the transition from early colonisation to mature reef community, providing the evidence base for the production hypothesis at North Sea scale. The Appendix contains the full species lists and acoustic trawl survey methodology.

- **BSH (Bundesamt für Seeschifffahrt und Hydrographie). (2020).** *Standard: Investigation of the Impacts of Offshore Wind Turbines on the Marine Environment (StUK4), 4th Edition.* Bundesamt für Seeschifffahrt und Hydrographie, Hamburg. Available at bsh.de. The German Federal Maritime and Hydrographic Agency's investigation standard defines the mandatory ecological survey programme for German offshore wind farms from pre-construction baseline through post-construction monitoring. It covers fish, benthic invertebrates, seabirds, harbour porpoises, and migratory bats. StUK4 is the most detailed national standard for offshore wind environmental monitoring in European waters and has been adopted, in adapted form, by several neighbouring countries. Appendix 2 contains the impulsive noise standard (160 dB SEL / 190 dB peak at 750 m) with the methodological basis for both criteria.

- **Topham, E., & McMillan, D. (2017).** "Sustainable decommissioning of an offshore wind farm." *Renewable Energy*, 102, pp. 470–480. DOI: 10.1016/j.renene.2016.10.066. A systematic analysis of decommissioning costs, methods, and sustainability considerations for the first generation of offshore wind farms. The paper presents a cost model structured by component type (turbine, foundation, cable, monitoring) and decommissioning method (full removal, partial removal, reuse or repowering), validated against available data from decommissioned onshore and near-shore installations and extrapolated to larger offshore facilities using scaling relationships derived from North Sea construction industry data. The analysis covers both the direct financial cost and the embodied carbon of each decommissioning pathway, providing a sustainability argument for repowering and partial removal that goes beyond the financial minimisation case.

*Kaan stayed on the Doktor Jan Mielke until seventeen hundred, when the CTV came back from the OSS to collect him.*

*By then, Zofia had completed the T14 and T17 surveys and started on T09 — the one with the gearbox bearing on Erik's watch list. She did not remark on the coincidence. She had already noted the amber health index on the OSS dashboard when she checked the farm's monitoring systems that morning, and she had planned to survey T09's foundation community anyway. The bearing and the reef were independent subjects that happened to share a turbine.*

*He stood on the aft deck while the ROV was recovered and thought about the distance between the two problems. One was about what was wearing out inside the machine. The other was about what was growing on the outside of it. Both required continuous monitoring. Both produced evidence that changed the maintenance and management decision — in one case, the bearing replacement schedule; in the other, eventually, the decommissioning strategy. Both had taken eighteen months to become clearly readable. Both had been invisible to the SCADA system that measured megawatts and availability.*

*The CTV bumped against the aft fender and the deckhand threw a line. Kaan picked up his bag.*

*"The reef data will go to the regulator in February," Zofia said from the survey deck. She was labelling sample vials without looking up. "They will want to know what species are present and in what abundance. They will not yet ask what should be done about it at decommissioning. That question is still twenty years away." She placed a vial in the rack. "But someone will have to answer it."*

*He climbed onto the CTV. The turbines were turning steadily behind him in the afternoon light, and below each one — invisible from the surface, invisible from the SCADA screen, invisible from the compliance matrices that Zawadzki had reviewed in September — the reef was growing. Forty-one species at T14. Cod at T09. Kelp extending two metres into the water column on every foundation they had surveyed today.*

*That evening, after dinner, he read the forward chapter list in the FAT binder that Åsa had left on the commissioning room table in September — the list that described what came after operations, after optimisation, after the biological surveys. It said, in the margin in Anders's handwriting: floating, HVDC, hydrogen, 40 MW.*

*Those were not the next maintenance window. They were the next decade.*

*He turned off the light.*
