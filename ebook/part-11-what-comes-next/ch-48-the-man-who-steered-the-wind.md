# Chapter 48: The Man Who Steered the Wind

*The observation deck was empty at that hour.*

*Kaan had arrived on the first crew transfer vessel of the morning, before the day shift, with a travel coffee mug that had gone cold somewhere over the Øresund. The CTV captain had not recognised him until they were halfway across — the same run he had made two years earlier, on his first day, looking at the turbines as white lines on a grey horizon and not knowing what he was looking at. He had not explained any of this to the captain. Some stories are too long for a CTV crossing.*

*The helicopter pad light was still on. He walked around the back of the control building to the observation deck that faced north, where all thirty-four turbines were visible from the rail.*

*They were all turning.*

*Not at full capacity — the wind speed at hub height was perhaps eight or nine metres per second, which in Region 2 means the pitch angle is at minimum and the rotor is maximising its power coefficient rather than running at rated torque. He could estimate the rotational speed from two years of watching: approximately six revolutions per minute, which meant the blade tips were moving at approximately three hundred and fifty kilometres per hour. Faster than a Formula 1 car, as Morten had told him when they had stood at the blade root before the crane had lifted it into position. He had not known then what that comparison was supposed to make him feel.*

*He knew now.*

*Thirty-four rotors, each sweeping a disc four hundred and thirty-seven square metres across — he had done the calculation so many times he no longer needed to reach for it. Each extracting kinetic energy from a column of moving air that narrowed and slowed as it passed through the rotor, leaving a turbulent wake that spread and mixed with undisturbed air before reaching the next row. He could see the wake gradient from here, in the slightly faster rotation of the front turbines against the slightly slower rotation of the machines in rows three and four. A difference of perhaps twelve per cent in rotor speed, representing perhaps twenty per cent less power, because air carries its velocity deficit forward for seven, ten, twelve rotor diameters before the mixing restores it.*

*Signe had told him this, in a room full of monitor screens showing convergence plots, four days into his first month on the farm. The layout had taken six months of iterative optimisation. The improvement over a regular grid was one hundred and fifty million euros over thirty years. The difference between her result and the globally optimal result — a result she could not prove existed or find if it did — was unknown.*

*He had arrived knowing that wind turbines rotate and that rotating things generate electricity.*

*He understood now what the rotating thing was made of, why it was that shape, what the air was doing when it passed through it, what the electricity became when it left the turbine, where it went, what was built to carry it, who decided how much of it was worth how much, how a machine this large was kept running in a sea that did not ask permission, and what would need to be different about the next machine — the one that did not yet exist.*

*He had arrived knowing the answer to none of this.*

*He took a drink of cold coffee and looked north.*

---

Eight thousand years ago — approximately, in the way that historians are approximate about things that were not written down — people in the coastal lowlands between the Tigris and the Euphrates began to put reed sails on flat-bottomed boats. The physical logic is unambiguous even where the evidence is sparse: a sail is a device for extracting kinetic energy from moving air and converting it into motion. The people who built the first sails did not know they were doing fluid mechanics. They knew that the wind pushed the sail, and that a pushed boat moved faster than a rowed one, and that moving faster was better.

That observation — that the wind could be made to do something useful — is the oldest surviving engineering insight in the history of the discipline.

The windmills at Nashtifan in the Khorasan province of Iran were built sometime in the ninth or tenth century CE — approximately one thousand years ago. They are still standing. Some of them still operate, their vertical wooden rotor blades catching the hundred-and-twenty-day wind that blows from the north every summer, as it has blown for as long as the town has existed. The people who built them understood — with a precision they could not have articulated in mathematical terms — that a blade angled correctly to the wind would turn faster than a blade presented flat; that the angle had to be adjusted if the wind speed changed; that the whole structure had to be oriented into the prevailing wind or it would stop. They understood these things empirically, through generations of construction and observation and failure and modification. They were engineers. They did not have that word for themselves.

Between Nashtifan and the observation deck on which Kaan was standing, the following had happened: the spread of vertical-axis windmills across the Islamic world; the arrival of horizontal-axis post mills in twelfth-century England; the four-century Dutch refinement of the windmill from a simple grain mill to a precisely controllable machine with moveable fantail, spring sails, and variable-pitch blades; the steam engine, which made wind power temporarily irrelevant to industry; the first experiments with electricity from wind by Poul la Cour at the Askov Folk High School in 1891; the American farm windmill, which pumped water from aquifers across the Great Plains and made settlement possible in a landscape without rivers; the 1973 oil crisis, which gave governments a reason to fund research into alternatives; the Nibe A turbine in Denmark in 1979, among the first large-scale grid-connected wind turbines in the world; the California wind rush of the 1980s; the offshore wind industry, which began with eleven Bonus 450 kW turbines at Vindeby in 1991 and thirty years later produced machines thirty-three times larger than any machine built in California; and the cost reduction.

The cost reduction deserves its own sentence.

In 2010, the levelised cost of electricity from offshore wind was approximately two hundred and twenty dollars per megawatt-hour. By 2024, it was approximately eighty-four dollars per megawatt-hour. A sixty-two per cent reduction in fourteen years. In the same period, the cost of electricity from coal fell by approximately six per cent. The cost from combined-cycle gas fell by approximately fourteen per cent. The cost from offshore wind fell by sixty-two per cent, driven by turbine scaling — from two megawatts in 1991 to fifteen megawatts by 2022 — by learning-curve improvements in manufacturing and installation, by competitive procurement policy, and by the accumulated engineering knowledge of tens of thousands of engineers working on the same problem from different directions across two decades.

The cost reduction was not an accident. It was not luck. It was the result of understanding the physics deeply enough to know where the engineering had room to move, and then moving it.

*He thought about the people.*

*Not all of them — the complete accounting would take longer than the morning — but the ones whose phrases had stayed with him, the ones whose objects he could still picture, the ones who had taught him something he had not known he needed to know.*

*Morten, who had carried a decommissioned blade cross-section into a maintenance hall on trestles and let Kaan run his finger along the aerofoil camber before explaining a word of lift theory. The physical thing first. The formula as explanation, not as introduction. "The guys who designed this probably ran ten thousand BEM iterations before they got to this shape." He had been talking about the blade. He had been describing a principle of engineering work: the optimisation is real, and it is expensive, and the result of it is in your hands.*

*Anders, who had said almost nothing in the beginning and more as the months passed, in the manner of someone who believed that a question unasked was a question whose answer the student was not yet ready for. Who had pressed a coffee on Kaan in the mess room on the evening of Day One and said, unprompted and without preamble: "Before you touch anything in that substation, you are going to understand alternating current." And then, over the following weeks, taught it — not as a subject to be memorised but as a physical reality to be inhabited. The current that flowed in the cables was real. The reactive power was real. The phasor was a representation of something real. Anders had never let a formula stand without a physical meaning sentence attached.*

*Elif, with the mug. He had seen it three times in two years, each time in a different kitchen, each time with the same faint professional disdain — entirely non-hostile — for everyone who had not yet understood why reactive power was worth loving. The disdain was not unkind. It was the expression of someone who had learned something genuinely beautiful and was faintly impatient for everyone around them to catch up. He had caught up. He hoped she knew.*

*Maja, on the met mast platform at forty metres, pulling a Kestrel weather meter from her jacket pocket the way other people checked their phones. "The difference between data and knowledge is what happens between this instrument and your model." He had stood in front of two hundred engineers in Copenhagen two days ago and said something that had passed from Maja through Jonasz through two years of his own practice into a sentence he now considered his own. That was how the knowledge moved.*

*Pieter, carrying a transparent soil core sample — grey clay, brown sand, dark glacial till — through a conversation about foundations as if the layers of the seabed were a story you could hold in your hands. "You cannot fix what you cannot reach." He had been talking about foundations. He had been describing the irreversibility that governs every decision made before the concrete sets.*

*Johan Carlsson, from Västerås, who had brought a thermos of coffee from home because the platform machine was inadequate, and who had commanded a one-hundred-and-twenty-two MVAR step change live and let Kaan time it on his phone. Eighteen milliseconds. "The cable does not sleep." He had known the result before the test because he had done it before, in seven countries, because physics does not vary by geography.*

*Helena, who spoke in numbers that sounded like verdicts, who had stood on the observation deck for exactly twenty seconds in silence on her first physical visit — the only twenty seconds in two years he had seen her unable to find the correct financial term for what she was observing — before saying "Right. Conference room." The turbines had briefly, for twenty seconds, been something other than a cash flow.*

*Brigid, who had handed him two padlocks with his name written on them in her handwriting. The weight of them was specific. He could still feel it.*

*These were not the people he had expected to meet in this job. He had expected engineers. He had found engineers who were also teachers — who had been taught by engineers who were also teachers, going back in an unbroken chain to the people who had first understood that the wind could be made to do something useful.*

---

The physics of wind power has not changed since it was first correctly described.

Daniel Bernoulli published his theorem in 1738 — the relationship between fluid velocity and pressure that makes an aerofoil produce lift. Albert Betz derived the theoretical maximum efficiency of a wind rotor in 1919 and published it in 1926: no rotor operating in a free stream can extract more than fifty-nine point three per cent of the kinetic energy passing through its swept area. Michael Faraday demonstrated electromagnetic induction in 1831. Charles Steinmetz published the phasor method for AC circuit analysis in 1893. The cubic relationship between wind speed and power — the fact that a twenty-five per cent wind speed increase multiplies available power by nearly double — is a direct consequence of kinetic energy theory that has been settled since Newton.

These things were true before any person currently working in offshore wind was born. They will be true after the last person currently working in offshore wind has retired.

What changes is the engineering that applies them. The blade that turns Bernoulli into megawatts in 2024 is a different object from the blade that did the same in 1991 — in size, in material, in manufacturing precision, in the aerofoil database that shaped its cross-sections from root to tip. The substation that converts converter output into grid power is a different object from the transformer room at Vindeby in 1991 — in voltage level, in digital intelligence, in the communication protocols by which it monitors and reports on itself. But the physics connecting the blade to the substation to the grid is the same physics.

Understanding the physics does not mean knowing the answers. It means knowing which questions to ask. And it means that when the technology changes — when the fifteen-megawatt turbine is replaced by a forty-megawatt turbine, when the grid-following converter is replaced by a grid-forming one, when the AC export cable is replaced by HVDC — the engineer who understands the physics knows where the new technology fits in the existing framework and what it changes and, more importantly, what it does not.

The reason the long HVDC cable does not generate reactive power is the same reason the short AC cable does: one is DC and the other is AC, and reactive power is a consequence of alternating current in a capacitive circuit. The reason the grid-forming converter remains stable at low short-circuit ratios is the same reason the grid-following converter does not: one synthesises a voltage reference and the other requires one. The physics had not changed. The architecture had.

This is what Kaan had meant, on the conference stage in Copenhagen, when he had said: *the physics does not change at once*. Not as a slogan. As a practical engineering statement about where to anchor your knowledge when everything else is in motion.

*The typing indicator had appeared and disappeared twice before Anders responded.*

*Kaan had sent: "Someone asked me your question."*

*He had meant: the question that he himself had asked, two years ago, standing at the rail of a crew transfer vessel with thirty-four turbines on the horizon, not yet understanding what he was looking at. How do you know what to prioritise when everything is changing at once? The student in the third row — tall, with the orange safety vest still folded and creased from its packaging — had asked it more precisely and with less anxiety than Kaan had asked it, but it was the same question.*

*"What did you tell them?" Anders had written back.*

*"That the physics doesn't change at once."*

*A pause. Three minutes. He had watched the turbines from the observation deck and held his phone in his jacket pocket.*

*"That's correct. Not the whole answer, but it's a correct beginning."*

*He had smiled. It was the first time he had smiled that day — standing alone on a metal deck in the North Sea at seven in the morning with a cold coffee and thirty-four turbines behind him and two hundred engineers somewhere in Copenhagen already dispersing to their projects.*

*"What's the rest of the answer?"*

*The typing indicator had appeared, disappeared, appeared again.*

*"That's what the next two years are for."*

*He had put the phone away and looked at the turbines and thought: yes, that's right. That's exactly right.*

---

There is a phrase — it appears in several forms in the culture of people who work with large technical systems — that expresses something important about the knowledge required to do this work. The phrase is: *the machine does not fail the way you expect it to*.

A protection relay can be set correctly for the current grid and still contribute to a cascade if the grid's composition changes, because the setting that is correct for a high-inertia system becomes a source of instability when the inertia falls. A cable can pass every factory test and still produce unexpected reactive power in a configuration the tests did not model. A machine learning model can achieve excellent validation accuracy on historical data and still perform badly on a frontal passage that the training years did not contain.

The work of a wind energy engineer in the 2020s is the work of finding these failures before they occur. Not by checking boxes on a compliance matrix — compliance matrices are important, but they test for what has happened before, not for what will happen first — but by understanding the physical and computational systems deeply enough to ask: what changes when conditions change? What assumption does this design make that will not hold in ten years? Where is the failure mode that the standard has not yet been written for?

This is not a new problem in engineering. It is the original problem. The Nashtifan windmills were optimised for one specific seasonal wind in one specific geography. The people who maintained them for a thousand years understood, empirically and in their bodies, what those mills required. The understanding was specific, local, and physical.

Modern offshore wind engineering is specific, distributed, and physical and mathematical and data-driven and ecological simultaneously. The person who understands it does not have the knowledge of a mechanical engineer, or the knowledge of an electrical engineer, or the knowledge of a data scientist, or the knowledge of a marine ecologist. They have some knowledge of all of these disciplines and the specific knowledge of how they interact at the point where air, water, metal, and current meet.

This is what Kaan had become. He was not entirely sure when it had happened. There had been no moment of transition. There had been, instead, a series of moments where a thing that had been opaque became transparent — the reactive power measurement that connected Elif's coffee-room explanation to Johan's live test to Ingrid's compliance table; the protection coordination calculation that connected Sigrid's relay timing to the arc energy the cable could absorb if the relay failed to the cost of the switchgear replacement that would follow. The connections were not revealed to him. He had built them, one relationship at a time, from the bottom up.

*He thought about what the farm was, viewed from outside the technical knowledge.*

*A marine ecologist from the University of Gdańsk had shown him, in Month Eighteen, underwater footage from a ROV pass around the base of Turbine Fourteen. The scour protection rock — the granite blocks placed around the monopile during installation to prevent seabed erosion — had been colonised. Forty-one species. Mytilus edulis covering the lower courses of the monopile in dense blue-black mats. Laminaria saccharina waving in the gentle current at five metres depth. And, near the base of the rock pile, moving slowly through the kelp fronds, the unmistakable heavy-bodied silhouette of Gadus morhua — Atlantic cod.*

*The cod had not been engineered. It had not been consulted. It had arrived because the scour protection rock provided what the sandy Baltic seafloor did not: vertical structure, surface complexity, a place for mussels and kelp to establish, and therefore a place for the things that ate mussels and kelp to feed. The reef was a consequence of the engineering. Nobody had designed it. It had happened when the engineering was solved well enough that something else could start.*

*He thought that this was probably the right way to think about what the farm was, at full resolution. Not the turbines. Not the cables. Not the SCADA system or the protection relays or the PPC. Those were the parts that had required engineering decisions. The farm, in its entirety, was the sum of a thousand decisions made by a hundred engineers across a decade: decisions about layout and foundation diameter and cable routing and commissioning sequence and protection settings and maintenance contracts. The decisions were imperfect. The engineering had not optimised for cod. But the decisions had been made with enough care, and enough physical understanding of what they would do, that the result was a structure the sea could build something on.*

*The commercial operation certificate was in a filing system in Warsaw.*

*The reef was at forty-one metres depth, twelve nautical miles north of the coast of the Polish Baltic.*

*Both of them were the result of the same engineering.*

---

The title of this book is the title of this chapter. It requires explanation, because the wind cannot be steered.

The wind does what the physics of the atmosphere requires. It flows from high pressure to low pressure. It is deflected by the Coriolis force and by surface roughness and by the thermal gradients between sea and land. It carries turbulence from obstacles it encountered twenty kilometres upwind. It arrives at the turbine rotor having been shaped by every physical feature between its origin over the Norwegian Sea and the blade leading edge, and the turbine cannot change any of this.

What the turbine can do is convert the energy the wind carries into electrical power, at a rate determined by the rotor's position relative to the wind, the blade pitch angle, the generator control, the converter output, the grid connection settings, and the reactive power compensation. What the control engineer can do is optimise all of these parameters simultaneously, in real time, to extract the maximum power the wind permits while remaining within every constraint the machine, the grid, and the regulations impose.

The wind is not steered. It passes through. What is steered — continuously, in response to every change in wind speed and direction and frequency and voltage — is the machine that stands in the wind's path and catches what it can.

The man who steered the wind is the man who learned to control everything except the wind itself, and found that it was enough.

This is not a small thing. The constraints are genuinely numerous and the physics, fully understood, is genuinely sophisticated. The knowledge required to connect what a blade does to what a protection relay must do to what a financial model must assume is distributed across disciplines that do not normally speak to each other, and it is accumulated over years of practice that cannot be fully compressed into any single book, including this one.

But it is learnable.

The physics can be understood by anyone willing to sit with a formula long enough to ask: what does this variable mean physically? What happens to the result if I double this quantity? What assumption is the model making that might not hold in the next situation I encounter? The engineering can be understood by anyone willing to follow one component — a cable, a transformer, a relay, a SCADA message — from its physical behaviour through the mathematics that describe it to the standards that govern it to the project decision that determined its rating. The system can be understood by anyone willing to look at a farm from the outside — from an observation deck, from a financial model, from a marine ecologist's survey vessel — and ask: what is this, all together, and what does it do to everything around it?

Kaan had arrived at these questions through two years of being in the middle of the problem. He had not arrived with them. He had been given them, one at a time, by people who understood that the middle of the problem was the only place from which the questions became visible.

This book is an attempt to give the reader those questions before they need them.

*There were three of them — one he recognised from the O&M team, two he did not — and they arrived with the particular efficient energy of people who had done this many times and were not surprised by thirty-four turbines turning in a Baltic dawn, because turning was expected, because an alarm was news and a full-output farm on a good wind day was simply confirmation that the engineering was continuing to work. Some things do not require explanation between engineers who have worked with the same machine.*

*He looked at his phone. A message from the student with the orange vest, sent at 06:43 that morning:*

*"I looked up the cubic power law. The derivation is simpler than I expected."*

*He had written back, on the CTV, before the phone had gone back in his pocket:*

*"It always is, once you know the physics."*

*He thought about this now.*

*Anders had said almost exactly the same thing to him, once — sometime in the middle of the months on the OSS, in a corridor conversation he had not known was important at the time. "It always makes sense when you understand it from first principles. The difficulty is that you have to understand it before you can understand it."*

*The circularity had frustrated him at the time. He thought he understood it now. Learning physics is not linear. You encounter a formula before you understand it, apply it before you understand why, make a mistake that reveals the assumption you were not aware of, and then you understand. The understanding comes after the encounter, not before it. This is not a flaw in the pedagogy. It is the only way the knowledge can be transmitted: you have to be in the middle of the problem before the physics becomes legible.*

*The student with the orange vest was somewhere in that middle stage now. He had found the cubic derivation and it had made sense. Next month, or next year, he would encounter a situation where the cubic law produced a result that seemed wrong, and the investigation of why it seemed wrong would reveal an assumption he had not known he was making, and after that he would understand the cubic law in a different way than he had understood it from the derivation. This had happened to Kaan at least twelve times in two years. Each time, the formula he had thought he understood became something larger.*

*He wondered briefly whether Anders, thirty years into his career, was still having this experience.*

*He suspected the answer was yes.*

*Somewhere at the base of Turbine Fourteen, forty-one metres below the surface of the Baltic Sea, a cod was moving slowly through a kelp frond in the current.*

*A GOOSE message had just travelled from a protective relay on the OSS busbar to the circuit breaker it supervised: a routine status update, generated every ten thousand milliseconds, confirming that the breaker remained closed and that all was well. The message had carried sixty-four bytes of data. It had arrived in four milliseconds.*

*Eight thousand years of engineering, accumulated one insight at a time, by people who had understood — each of them, in their own era and language — that the wind could be made to do something useful.*

*Four milliseconds.*

*He took the last drink of the cold coffee, set the mug on the deck rail, and went inside to start work.*

## Key Takeaways

- **The engineering is learnable.** Wind energy requires knowledge spanning aerodynamics, power systems, marine engineering, data science, ecology, and finance. No single engineer masters all of it fully. But anyone willing to follow the physics from one domain into the next will find that the disciplines connect, because they are all describing different aspects of the same physical system.

- **The physics does not change at once.** Technologies change, regulations change, economics change. But the reason a cable generates reactive power has not changed since the cable's capacitance was first measured. Engineers who understand the physics can navigate change; engineers who only understand the current technology must relearn every decade.

- **The cost reduction was not an accident.** The sixty-two per cent reduction in offshore wind levelised cost between 2010 and 2024 was the result of understanding the physics well enough to know where the engineering had room to move, and then moving it — in thousands of projects, by tens of thousands of engineers, over two decades.

- **What you steer is the machine, not the wind.** The wind does what the atmosphere requires. The engineer's work is to control everything except the wind itself — the conversion, the transmission, the grid connection, the protection, the forecast — and make that sufficient.

- **There will always be a next question.** The student who asks how to prioritise when everything is changing at once is asking the same question every engineer asks at the beginning. The answer is the same: understand the physics first. Not because the physics gives you the answer, but because it tells you where to look.
